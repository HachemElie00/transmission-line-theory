Impedance matching

Every chapter so far has been describing what a mismatched line does. This one undoes it. Two tools — a length of line, and a stub — are enough to turn any load that absorbs power into a perfect match, and the Smith chart turns the design into a two-step construction.

Assumes: chapters 09 and 10 — stubs, and reading the chart.

What matching buys

The goal is stated in one line: make the impedance seen at the input equal $Z_0$, so that $\Gamma = 0$ and no wave returns. Four things follow from achieving it.

BenefitWhy
All the power arrivesthe reflected fraction $|\Gamma|^2$ goes to zero
Length stops matteringwith no reflected wave there is nothing to rotate, so $Z_{\text{in}} = Z_0$ at every distance
No voltage peaksthe standing-wave maximum $1+|\Gamma|$ collapses to 1, so nothing is over-stressed
The source behavesamplifiers are specified into a known load; a reflection can detune or damage them

The second row is the one that changes how a system is built. An unmatched line is a component whose behaviour depends on its own length, so every cable becomes part of the design. Match it and the cable is just a cable again.

The constraint that shapes every method

A matching network must be built from lossless elements. Putting a resistor in to soak up the reflection would work, and would also throw away the power you were trying to deliver. So every technique below uses only line lengths, stubs, inductors and capacitors — things that store energy rather than consume it.

When the load is real: the quarter-wave transformer

Chapter 09 already solved this case. A real load $R_L$ matches to $Z_0$ through a quarter-wave section of $Z_1 = \sqrt{Z_0 R_L}$, and that is the whole design.

A complex load can be brought into that case first. Chapter 08 showed that the impedance is purely real at exactly two kinds of point on the line: at a voltage minimum it equals $Z_0/S$, at a voltage maximum $S Z_0$. They alternate every $\lambda/4$, so there is always one of each within the first half wavelength, and either will do.

  1. Find the distance $d$ from the load to the first voltage minimum, or to the first maximum.
  2. There the line looks like a pure resistance: $R = Z_0/S$ at the minimum, $R = S Z_0$ at the maximum.
  3. Insert a quarter-wave section of $Z_1 = \sqrt{Z_0 R}$ at that point — $Z_1 = Z_0/\sqrt{S}$ at the minimum, $Z_1 = Z_0\sqrt{S}$ at the maximum.

On an impedance chart both points are where the constant-SWR circle crosses the horizontal axis: on the right at $r = S$, the voltage maximum, and on the left at $r = 1/S$, the minimum. Plot $z_L$, read the wavelengths-toward-generator scale on the radius through it, travel clockwise to whichever crossing you want, and read the scale again. The difference is $d$, and the value at the crossing is $R/Z_0$. The workbench's practice mode takes you through it.

On an admittance chart — the axis reads the other way

The crossings are the same two points and $d$ comes out the same, but the axis now reads conductance, and the roles swap. Where it reads $g = S$, the impedance there is $z = 1/S$, so that crossing is the voltage minimum; where it reads $g = 1/S$ is the maximum.

Convert before choosing the section, $R = Z_0/g$: either $Z_1 = Z_0/\sqrt{S}$ or $Z_1 = Z_0\sqrt{S}$, as above. Reading $g$ as if it were $r$ gives the right number for the wrong point, and a transformer $S$ times off.

The two choices differ in practice, not in principle. One section is lower than $Z_0$ and one higher by the same factor $\sqrt{S}$, so pick whichever is easier to build — a wider track or a narrower one, a cable you can buy — or whichever needs the shorter $d$. The workbench offers both.

It works, and for a fixed frequency it is clean. The drawback is practical: $Z_1$ comes out as whatever the algebra demands, and on microstrip that means a track of a particular width you may not want, or on coax a cable impedance nobody sells. Stub matching avoids that entirely, because every piece of it is the same line you already have.

Single-stub matching

The idea is two moves, and each move fixes one of the two numbers you need to fix.

$$\textbf{1. Travel} \;\; d \;\text{ along the line until } \; y = 1 + jb$$ $$\textbf{2. Add a shunt stub } \; \text{of susceptance } -b \;\Rightarrow\; y_{\text{total}} = 1$$ the line fixes the real part; the stub cancels what is left

Two points of technique are worth stating plainly. We work in admittance because the stub goes in parallel, and parallel elements add admittances — that is the only reason chapter 10 bothered with the overlay. And the stub is purely reactive, so it can change $b$ without touching $g$; it cannot fix the real part, which is why the travel has to come first.

Full derivation — where to stop, in closed form

Write $\Gamma(d) = u + jv$ with $u^2 + v^2 = |\Gamma|^2 = m^2$, constant on a lossless line. The normalised admittance there is

$$y = \frac{1-\Gamma}{1+\Gamma} = \frac{(1-m^2) - j2v}{(1+u)^2 + v^2}$$

Setting the real part to 1 gives a condition of striking simplicity:

$$\frac{1-m^2}{(1+u)^2+v^2} = 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; 1-m^2 = 1 + 2u + m^2 \;\Longrightarrow\; \boxed{u = -m^2}$$

So you must travel until the real part of $\Gamma$ equals $-|\Gamma|^2$. Since $|\Gamma|$ is fixed, that is a vertical line cutting the constant-SWR circle in two places — hence two solutions per half wavelength:

$$v = \pm m\sqrt{1-m^2}$$

The distance follows from how far $\Gamma$ must rotate clockwise from $\theta_L$ to reach that point, at $2\beta$ per unit length. And the leftover susceptance is

$$b = \frac{-2v}{1-m^2} = \mp\frac{2m}{\sqrt{1-m^2}}$$

Finally the stub length. A shorted stub has $y = -j\cot\beta l_s$ and an open one $y = +j\tan\beta l_s$, so setting either equal to $-jb$ gives

$$\text{shorted:}\;\; \cot\beta l_s = b \qquad\qquad \text{open:}\;\; \tan\beta l_s = -b$$

Every quantity is closed-form. Nothing here needs iteration, and the chart below evaluates exactly these expressions.

What the g = 1 circle is, and why you aim at it

Normalised admittance splits as $y = g + jb$, where $g$ is the conductance and $b$ the susceptance — the admittance counterparts of $r$ and $x$. The $g = 1$ circle is simply the set of all points whose conductance is 1, whatever their susceptance. It is a circle for the same reason the constant-$r$ curves are: chapter 10 showed the map from impedance to $\Gamma$ turns straight grid lines into circles, and admittance is that same map half a turn round.

Geometrically it is the $r = 1$ circle rotated 180°. It therefore passes through the centre of the chart, and runs from $\Gamma = 0$ out to $\Gamma = -1$.

It is the target because of what a stub can and cannot do:

ElementChangesLeaves aloneMoves you along
Length of lineboth $g$ and $b$$|\Gamma|$the constant-SWR circle
Shunt stub$b$ only$g$a constant-$g$ circle

The destination is the centre, $y = 1 + j0$, which sits on the $g = 1$ circle. Since the stub cannot change $g$, you must already have $g = 1$ before you add it — and the only tool that can change $g$ is the length of line. Hence the order: travel until you land on that circle, then let the stub carry you along it to the centre.

On the chart the construction is therefore two arcs. Rotate clockwise along the constant-SWR circle until you strike the $g = 1$ circle; then ride that circle to the centre. The first arc is the length of line; the second is the stub.

On a plain impedance chart, which is what you will usually have in front of you, $g$ is not printed. The same design is done with chapter 10's other convention: turn the point instead of the grid. Step by step:

  1. Plot the load. Normalise, $z_L = Z_L/Z_0$, mark it, and draw the constant-SWR circle through it, centred on the chart.
  2. Turn it half a turn. $y_L$ is diametrically opposite $z_L$ on the same circle. From here on, read every printed curve as admittance: the circles marked $r$ are now $g$, the arcs marked $x$ are now $b$, and the upper half of the chart holds $b > 0$, which is capacitive.
  3. Read the starting position. Extend the radius through $y_L$ to the wavelengths-toward-generator scale and read it.
  4. Travel to g = 1. Go clockwise round the constant-SWR circle until it meets the circle marked $r = 1$, which for a turned point is $g = 1$. It meets it twice in every half turn, and either crossing is a design. Read the scale again: $d$ is the second reading minus the first, plus $0.5\lambda$ if that comes out negative.
  5. Read what is left. The arc through the crossing gives $y = 1 + jb$. The stub must supply $-jb$.
  6. Size the stub. A stub is its own line, ending in a short or an open, so its input sits on the rim, $|\Gamma| = 1$. Read as admittance, a short ($y \to \infty$) is the right-hand end of the horizontal axis, at $0.25\lambda$ on the scale, and an open ($y = 0$) is the left-hand end, at $0$. Start at the stub's end, go clockwise round the rim to the arc that reads $-b$, and read the scale: the stub length is that reading minus the starting one, plus $0.5\lambda$ if negative.
  7. Check, and turn back. $1 + jb - jb = 1$, the centre. The centre is its own half turn, so $y = 1$ there is also $z = 1$: the line is matched as an impedance too, and turning back changes nothing.

The figure below computes the same $d$ and stub length for any load, and the workbench's practice mode checks each of these moves as you make it.

On an admittance chart — the same design without the half turn

An admittance chart prints the grid already turned: its circles are constant $g$, its arcs constant $b$, and the point does not move. Find $g_L + jb_L$ on that grid. It lands where $z_L$ would on an impedance chart, so the wavelength scale reads exactly as it would for the load itself.

  1. Draw the constant-SWR circle through $y_L$ and read the wavelengths-toward-generator scale there.
  2. Travel clockwise to the printed $g = 1$ circle, which passes through the centre and the left-hand end of the axis. Read the scale again; the difference is $d$.
  3. Read $b$ on the arc through the crossing. The stub must add $-b$.
  4. On this grid the short ($y \to \infty$) is the left-hand end of the axis, at $0$ on the scale, and the open is the right-hand end, at $0.25\lambda$. Go clockwise round the rim from the stub's end to the arc reading $-b$; the scale difference is the stub length.

Both routes give the same $d$ and the same stub length. Turning the point shifts every scale reading by the same $\lambda/4$, and differences cancel it. The mistake to avoid is doing both: turning the point and reading an admittance grid applies the minus sign twice, and what you read is $z_L$ again.

The combined chart of chapter 10 prints both grids at once. Read $g$ and $b$ off the admittance family and leave the point where it is. The workbench does this with solve on: admittance chart.

The last readout is the check that matters: after the stub, $y = 1.00 + j0.00$ exactly, for every load you can set. Press Other solution to see the second intersection — both match perfectly, and choosing between them is a practical question. The shorter travel usually wins, but a stub length near zero or near $\lambda/2$ is awkward to fabricate, so the other branch sometimes gives a more comfortable pair of dimensions.

To design one against a real chart — with the wavelength scales round the rim, the stub drawn as its own arc, and the series-stub, transformer, L-network and double-stub designs for the same load one menu choice away — use the workbench.

The series-stub version

Everything above has a mirror image in impedance instead of admittance, for the same reason $z$ and $y$ are mirror images of each other: a series element adds impedance, so it needs a constant-$r$ circle through the centre to ride, exactly as a shunt element needed the constant-$g$ one.

$$\textbf{1. Travel} \;\; d \;\text{ along the line until } \; z = 1 + jx$$ $$\textbf{2. Add a series stub } \; \text{of reactance } -x \;\Rightarrow\; z_{\text{total}} = 1$$ the same two moves, with $g = 1$ replaced by $r = 1$ and a shunt stub replaced by a series one

Repeating the single-stub derivation with $z = (1+\Gamma)/(1-\Gamma)$ in place of $y$ flips exactly one sign. Setting $\operatorname{Re}(z) = 1$ gives $u = +m^2$ this time, not $-m^2$ — travel until $\Gamma$'s real part equals $+|\Gamma|^2$, the vertical line that cuts the constant-SWR circle on the other side of the chart. The rest repeats unchanged: two crossings, two designs, and a leftover reactance

$$x = \frac{2v}{1-m^2}, \qquad v = \pm m\sqrt{1-m^2}$$

cancelled by a stub presenting $-x$. A shorted series stub looks like $Z = jZ_0\tan\beta l_s$ at its base and an open one $Z = -jZ_0\cot\beta l_s$ — the reverse of the shunt case, because a stub's own input admittance and input impedance swap which one is $\tan$ and which is $-\cot$. Setting either equal to $-x$ gives the length directly:

$$\text{shorted:}\;\; \tan\beta l_s = -x \qquad\qquad \text{open:}\;\; \cot\beta l_s = x$$

On an impedance chart nothing has to be turned, since a series element adds impedance:

  1. Plot the load $z_L$, draw its constant-SWR circle, and read the wavelengths-toward-generator scale on the radius through it.
  2. Travel to r = 1. Go clockwise to the circle marked $r = 1$; again there are two crossings. Read the scale there; the difference is $d$.
  3. Read what is left. The arc through the crossing gives $z = 1 + jx$. The stub must present $-x$.
  4. Size the stub. A shorted stub starts at $z = 0$, the left-hand end of the axis, at $0$ on the scale; an open one at $z \to \infty$, the right-hand end, at $0.25\lambda$. Go clockwise round the rim to the arc reading $-x$; the scale difference is the stub length.

The workbench's practice mode runs the same four moves.

On an admittance chart — turning the point the other way

This is the shunt-stub case reflected. A series element adds impedance, so on an admittance chart the point has to be turned half a turn before the grid can be read as impedance. Find $y_L$ on the admittance grid and turn it to $z_L$. The circle marked $g = 1$ now reads $r = 1$, and the arcs read $x$.

Travel clockwise to that circle, read $x$ off the arc through the crossing, and size the stub from its end. The ends swap with the turn: read as impedance, a short ($z = 0$) is the right-hand end of the axis and an open the left. As before, $d$ and the stub length are scale differences, so they come out the same as on an impedance chart.

On the combined chart, read $r$ and $x$ off the impedance family and turn nothing.

The practical trade-off is physical, not mathematical: a series stub has to be spliced into the line rather than tapped onto it, which is routine in coax and slotline but awkward in microstrip, where cutting the conductor to insert a stub costs a connector or a via. That is why microstrip designs default to shunt stubs and coax designs often do not.

Choosing between the four designs

A single shunt stub has two positions, and at each the stub can be shorted or open. All four match perfectly at $f_0$. Away from it they differ, and by a lot, because every length in the design is an electrical length that scales with frequency: the longer the line and the stub, the faster the match walks away.

The pattern is general: the design with the shortest total length, travel plus stub, holds its match over the widest band, often by a factor of two against the longest. Chapter 08's Foster theorem says why: a longer stub stores more energy for the same reactance and changes it faster with frequency. When the shortest design needs an awkwardly short stub, the next shortest is the usual compromise, and the choice between short and open is then settled by the board: a short needs a via, an open needs Δl subtracted.

A tuner you do not rebuild: the double stub

The single stub has a flaw hiding in plain sight: the distance $d$ depends on the load. Change the load and the stub has to move, which is fine for a one-off design etched onto a board but useless for a tuner — a device meant to be adjusted by hand to match whatever is plugged into it. Fixing that means fixing the geometry and adjusting only the two stub lengths.

The double-stub tuner does exactly that: one stub sits at the load, a second sits a fixed distance $d$ further toward the generator — commonly $\lambda/8$ or $3\lambda/8$ — and only the two lengths are tuned. Nothing ever has to slide.

The construction follows from one fact already used above: a shunt stub changes $b$ but leaves $g$ untouched. So after stub 1, the point is still somewhere on the same constant-$g$ circle the load started on, $g = g_L$. The fixed length of line then carries that point to stub 2's location, rotating it by $2\beta d$ — and that point must land on $g = 1$ for stub 2 to finish the job.

  1. Work backward. Take the $g = 1$ circle and rotate it back toward the load by $2\beta d$ — the direction travel undoes. Call this the target circle.
  2. Find where it meets the load's circle. The target circle crosses $g = g_L$ at up to two points. Whichever susceptance stub 1 adds to land on one of them is stub 1's length.
  3. Let the fixed spacing do the rest. Travelling $d$ from either crossing lands exactly on $g = 1$, by construction.
  4. Stub 2 finishes it the same way a single stub would: cancel whatever susceptance is left.

On a plain impedance chart, with the point turned as for the single stub:

  1. Plot and turn. Plot $z_L$ and turn it half a turn to $y_L$. Read every printed curve as admittance from here on.
  2. Draw the target circle. Take the circle marked $r = 1$ and rotate it counter-clockwise, toward the load, by the spacing. A length $d$ turns the chart through $2\beta d$, which is $720^\circ \times d/\lambda$: $90^\circ$ for $d = \lambda/8$.
  3. Stub 1. It changes $b$ only, so $y_L$ moves along its own printed circle, the one marked $r = g_L$. Follow that circle to the target circle. If the two never meet, the load is in the forbidden region at this spacing. Read the arc through the crossing, $b_1$: stub 1 adds $b_1 - b_L$, and is sized from its end on the rim exactly as the single stub was.
  4. The spacing. From the crossing, travel clockwise by $720^\circ \times d/\lambda$ round the constant-SWR circle through that point. It is a new circle, because stub 1 changed $|\Gamma|$. You land on the circle marked $r = 1$, which reads $g = 1$.
  5. Stub 2. Read the arc there, $b_2$. Stub 2 adds $-b_2$ and finishes at the centre.

The workbench's practice mode draws the rotated target circle and the forbidden region for the spacing you set.

On an admittance chart — the target circle without the half turn

On an admittance grid the point is not turned. The target is the printed $g = 1$ circle, through the centre and the left-hand end of the axis, rotated counter-clockwise by the same $720^\circ \times d/\lambda$.

  1. Find $y_L$ on the grid and follow its own circle, $g = g_L$, to the rotated target circle. Read $b_1$ there; stub 1 adds $b_1 - b_L$.
  2. Travel the spacing clockwise round the constant-SWR circle through the crossing. You land on the printed $g = 1$ circle.
  3. Read $b_2$; stub 2 adds $-b_2$. Both stubs are sized from their ends on the rim, with the short at the left-hand end of the axis on this grid.

The forbidden region is easy to see on this grid. It is the inside of the printed circle $g = 1/\sin^2\beta d$, which is $g = 2$ for $\lambda/8$. That circle just touches the rotated target circle, so a load whose own $g$ circle lies inside it never reaches the target.

$$\operatorname{Re}(y_2) = \frac{g\left(1+t^2\right)}{\left(1-tb\right)^2 + t^2g^2}, \qquad t = \tan\beta d$$ setting this to 1 and solving for $b$, the susceptance just after stub 1, gives a quadratic with up to two real roots

That quadratic is where the flaw shows up. Its discriminant goes negative — no real $b$, hence no design — once

$$g > \frac{1}{\sin^2\beta d}$$ the forbidden region: loads this far from matched cannot be reached at this spacing, by any pair of stub lengths

The reason is the same fact the construction was built on: stub 1 cannot move $g$, so if the load's own circle $g = g_L$ never reaches the rotated target circle, no stub 1 length will drag it there. The fix is not a longer stub — it is a different spacing, or a length of ordinary line in front of stub 1 to move $g$ before the tuner ever sees it.

The limit depends on the spacing, and not monotonically: $1/\sin^2\beta d$ is 2 at both $d = \lambda/8$ and $d = 3\lambda/8$, but falls to 1 at $d = \lambda/4$, where every load with $g > 1$ is out of reach. That makes $\lambda/4$ the worst spacing available, and is why $\lambda/8$ and $3\lambda/8$ are the ones built in practice. Spacings close to 0 or $\lambda/2$ forbid almost nothing, but drive the stubs toward extreme lengths and narrow bandwidths.

Full derivation — the quadratic, and both stub values

After stub 1 the admittance at the load is $y_1 = g + jb$: the stub has added susceptance and left $g$ alone. A normalised admittance moves along the line by the same rule as a normalised impedance, so travelling the spacing $d$ toward the generator gives

$$y_2 = \frac{y_1 + jt}{1 + jt\,y_1} = \frac{g + j(b + t)}{(1 - tb) + jtg}, \qquad t = \tan\beta d$$

Multiply top and bottom by the conjugate of the denominator. The denominator becomes $(1-tb)^2 + t^2g^2$, and the real part of the numerator is $g(1-tb) + tg(b+t) = g(1+t^2)$, which is the boxed result. Setting it to 1:

$$(1 - tb)^2 = g(1+t^2) - t^2g^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad b = \frac{1 \pm \sqrt{g(1+t^2) - t^2g^2}}{t}$$

The square root is real only while $g(1+t^2) \ge t^2g^2$, that is $g \le (1+t^2)/t^2 = 1/\sin^2\beta d$ — the forbidden region, from nothing more than the discriminant. Each root is a design. Stub 1 must add $b - b_L$, the difference between where the load sits and where the root needs it.

With the real part fixed at 1 the denominator equals $g(1+t^2)$, and the imaginary part left at stub 2 is

$$\operatorname{Im}(y_2) = \frac{(b+t)(1-tb) - tg^2}{g(1+t^2)}$$

Stub 2 adds the negative of this. Both lengths then follow exactly as for a single shunt stub: $\cot\beta l = -b_{\text{stub}}$ for a short, $\tan\beta l = b_{\text{stub}}$ for an open.

To see the forbidden region and the rotated target circle drawn on the chart, use the workbench's double-stub method; its two designs are a click apart.

Out of the forbidden region

A load inside the double-stub tuner's forbidden region cannot be matched at that spacing, because stub 1 cannot change $g$. The cure the section above named is a length of ordinary line in front of stub 1. Travel turns the point round its constant-SWR circle, which changes $g$, and once the point is outside the forbidden disc the tuner works.

The added line has to be at least long enough to clear the disc, and travelling further round is harmless until the point comes back toward it. Since the constant-SWR circle always reaches the low-$g$ side of the chart within a quarter wavelength, some length up to $\lambda/4$ always works. This is also why practical tuners often have three stubs: the third does the same job without anyone cutting cable.

Below microwave: the L-network

Stubs need a length of line comparable with a wavelength. At 100 MHz that is a couple of metres, which no circuit board will carry, so lower frequencies use lumped matching instead: two reactive components in an L.

MoveElementOn the chart
Series L or Cadds reactancealong a constant-$r$ circle
Shunt L or Cadds susceptancealong a constant-$g$ circle

Matching is then a path from the load to the centre using only those two kinds of arc, and which element sits next to the load depends on the load. With the series element at the load you slide along the load's own constant-$r$ circle, needing it to reach $g = 1$, from where the shunt element carries you to the centre; that works when $r_L < 1$. With the shunt element at the load you slide along the load's constant-$g$ circle to $r = 1$ and the series element finishes; that works when $g_L < 1$.

At least one of the two orders always works, because both failing would need $r_L > 1$ and $g_L > 1$ at once — and $g_L = r_L/(r_L^2+x_L^2) > 1$ already forces $r_L > r_L^2 + x_L^2 \ge r_L^2$, so $r_L < 1$. The two cannot both fail. Whichever order works generally offers two roots, which is again why there are two solutions. The one load no L-network can reach is a purely reactive one, $r_L = 0$: it absorbs no power, so nothing lossless can match it.

On a plain impedance chart only the $r$ circles are printed, so the $g = 1$ circle has to be drawn by hand. It is the circle marked $r = 1$ turned half a turn, through the centre and the left-hand end of the axis. With that one auxiliary circle both orders can be worked:

  1. Series first ($r_L < 1$). Plot $z_L$. The series element moves it along its own printed circle, $r = r_L$. Follow that circle to the auxiliary circle; it crosses twice, once above the axis and once below. Read the arc there, $x_1$. The series element is $x_1 - x_L$.
  2. Turn that point half a turn. It lands on the circle marked $r = 1$, now read as $g = 1$. Read the arc, $b$. The shunt element is $-b$, and carries the point along $g = 1$ to the centre.
  3. Shunt first ($g_L < 1$). Plot $z_L$ and turn it to $y_L$. The shunt element moves it along the printed circle through it, read as $g = g_L$. Follow that circle to the auxiliary circle and read the arc, $b_1$. The shunt element is $b_1 - b_L$.
  4. Turn back half a turn. The point lands on the circle marked $r = 1$. Read the arc, $x$. The series element is $-x$.

The workbench works it the same way, half turns included, both when it shows a solution and in practice mode, where it also draws the circle the first element rides.

On a combined chart — no half turns at all

The combined chart of chapter 10 prints both families, and the L-network is where it earns its place. Nothing is turned. A series element moves the point along a constant-$r$ circle, a shunt element along a constant-$g$ circle, and both target circles, $r = 1$ and $g = 1$, are already printed.

  1. Series first. From $z_L$ along $r = r_L$ to the $g = 1$ circle, reading the series reactance off the impedance arcs. Then along $g = 1$ to the centre, reading the shunt susceptance off the admittance arcs.
  2. Shunt first. From $z_L$ along $g = g_L$ to the $r = 1$ circle, reading the susceptance off the admittance arcs. Then along $r = 1$ to the centre, reading the reactance off the impedance arcs.

Every element value is the difference between the arc readings at the two ends of its move, and the signs follow the table below. On an admittance-only chart the same construction runs with the roles swapped: the $g$ circles are printed, and the $r = 1$ circle is the one drawn by hand. The workbench's both grid is the combined chart.

Full derivation — both orders in closed form

Work in normalised quantities, $z_L = r + jx_L$. Series element at the load ($r < 1$): add $jx$ to get $z_1 = r + j(x_L + x)$, then require the shunt element to finish, which needs $\operatorname{Re}(1/z_1) = 1$:

$$\frac{r}{r^2 + (x_L + x)^2} = 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = \pm\sqrt{r(1-r)} - x_L, \qquad b = \pm\sqrt{\frac{1-r}{r}}$$

with the signs taken together. The square roots are real exactly when $r \le 1$, which is the condition for this order.

Shunt element at the load ($g_L < 1$): add $jb$ across the load, then require $\operatorname{Re}(z_1) = 1$ so the series element can finish. Solving the resulting quadratic in $b$,

$$b = \frac{x_L \pm \sqrt{r}\,\sqrt{r^2 + x_L^2 - r}}{r^2 + x_L^2}, \qquad x = \frac{1}{b} + \frac{x_L}{r} - \frac{1}{b\,r}$$

The inner root is real when $r^2 + x_L^2 \ge r$, which is $g_L \le 1$ written in terms of $z_L$. These are the standard L-section results (Pozar, section 5.1) in normalised form; every pair, assembled back into the circuit, returns $z = 1$ exactly.

The trade-off against stubs is worth naming. Lumped parts are tiny at low frequency and hopeless at high frequency, where their own parasitics dominate and the "capacitor" stops being one. Stubs are exact and repeatable at microwave and absurdly large below it. The crossover sits around a gigahertz, which is why boards in that band often use both.

The Q of a lumped match

An L-network matching $R_{\text{lo}}$ to $R_{\text{hi}}$ has no freedom: its two element values, and therefore its Q, are fixed by the ratio. In the middle of the network, where the series and shunt elements meet, the node Q is

$$Q = \sqrt{\frac{R_{\text{hi}}}{R_{\text{lo}}} - 1}, \qquad X_s = QR_{\text{lo}}, \qquad B_p = \frac{Q}{R_{\text{hi}}}$$ the series reactance sits on the low side, the shunt susceptance across the high side; the bandwidth is roughly $2/Q$

A third element buys freedom, in either direction. A π or T network is two L-sections back to back through a virtual resistance lower or higher than both ends; that raises Q above the L-network's, narrowing the band, which is what a filter-like match wants. Two L-sections in cascade through an intermediate resistance between the ends, ideally $\sqrt{R_{\text{lo}}R_{\text{hi}}}$, do the opposite: each step is a smaller ratio, so each has a lower Q, and the band widens.

On the chart of chapter 10 the three paths are visible as arcs: the cascade stays inside a lower constant-Q arc than the single L, the π network swings out past it. Choosing a lumped match is choosing which arc to stay inside.

Real components lose some of the power. An inductor or capacitor with unloaded quality factor $Q_u$ dissipates, at the node Q $Q_n$ the network imposes on it, about $Q_n/Q_u$ of the power passing through. An L-network has two such elements, so

$$\text{insertion loss} \approx 10\log_{10}\!\left(1 + \frac{2Q_n}{Q_u}\right)\ \text{dB}$$

For a 4:1 match ($Q_n = 1.73$) with components of $Q_u = 50$ that is about 0.3 dB. The same formula says why high-Q matches with ordinary components are lossy, and why the wideband cascade is also the low-loss one.

A complex source. When the generator's own impedance $Z_s$ is complex, maximum power needs the network to present $Z_s^*$ to it, not $Z_0$. On a chart normalised to $Z_0$ the target is then the point $z_s^*$ rather than the centre, and every construction in this chapter runs unchanged with that point as its destination. An amplifier's output match is designed exactly this way.

Nothing matches over all frequencies

Every network here is designed at one frequency. Move away from it and the electrical lengths change, the reactances change, and the match degrades — exactly as chapter 09's transformer did.

This is not a limitation of any particular technique. There is a theoretical bound, the Bode–Fano limit, which says that for a given load the total match quality available across all frequencies is fixed: you may trade a deeper match over a narrow band against a shallower one over a wide band, but the budget cannot be enlarged.

$$\int_0^\infty \ln\frac{1}{|\Gamma(\omega)|}\,d\omega \;\le\; \frac{\pi}{RC}$$ for a parallel RC load — the area under the curve is bought and paid for

The engineering consequence is simple to state. If you need more bandwidth, use more sections — two stubs, a multi-section transformer, a taper — and accept the extra size. A perfect match over an infinite band is not difficult, it is impossible, and knowing that saves a great deal of wasted effort.

The bound, in numbers

For a load made of a resistance $R$ in parallel with a capacitance $C$, the Bode–Fano integral above has a clean consequence. The best any lossless network can do over a band $\Delta\omega$ is a constant reflection $\Gamma_m$ inside it and total reflection outside, and putting that rectangle into the integral gives

$$\Gamma_m \ge \exp\!\left(-\frac{\pi}{\Delta\omega RC}\right) = \exp\!\left(-\frac{\pi}{Q_L\,\Delta\omega/\omega_0}\right), \qquad Q_L = \omega_0RC$$ the load’s own Q sets the floor: for a wider band you must accept a worse match, and no network gets below the curve

The gap between the two curves is what a better network can still win: a multi-element match can crowd towards the floor, never cross it. And the floor itself rises steeply with $Q_L$. A load with a large reactive part stores a lot of energy for the power it absorbs, and no arrangement of further lossless parts can make it absorb well over a wide band. That is the quantitative form of the warning above: the budget is set by the load.

Where this leaves you

The chain is complete. A changing electric field makes a magnetic one, which makes an electric one a little further along, and the disturbance walks. Confine it between two conductors and the same physics reads as a ladder of $L$ and $C$. Terminate that ladder in the wrong impedance and a wave comes back. Two waves interfere into a standing pattern; that pattern makes the impedance depend on where you stand; and that dependence, drawn on a disc, is the chart you just used to cancel the whole thing out.

Every step was derived rather than asserted, and nothing was used before it was earned. The factors of two that looked arbitrary in chapter 07 were round trips. The circles on the chart were a Möbius map of straight lines. The quarter wave that inverts an impedance is the half turn that converts impedance to admittance.

What comes after

The natural next topics are waveguides, where the TEM assumption finally fails and modes appear; antennas, where the line is deliberately mismatched to free space; and scattering parameters, which generalise $\Gamma$ to networks with more than one port. All three assume exactly what you have just built.

Self-check

Three questions to finish on.

1. Why must the travel along the line come before the stub, rather than after?

A purely reactive shunt element adds $jb$ and cannot alter $g$ at all — on the chart it moves you along a constant-$g$ circle. Since the target is $y = 1$, you must already be on the $g = 1$ circle before the stub can finish the job, and travelling along the line is the only move available that changes $g$.

2. Why are there always two single-stub solutions?

Travelling along the line moves you round a circle of fixed radius, and that circle cuts the $g = 1$ circle twice — algebraically, $v = \pm m\sqrt{1-m^2}$. Both give a perfect match; they differ in the distance and in the sign of the leftover $b$, hence in the stub length. Either stub type can be used with either solution.

3. Could you match a load with a resistor instead, and would anything be wrong with that?

A resistive pad genuinely reduces $|\Gamma|$ at the input, and is sometimes used deliberately to protect a source from a badly behaved load. But it achieves this by absorbing the reflection, which defeats the usual purpose: the power you were trying to deliver ends up as heat. That is exactly why every network in this chapter is built only from elements that store energy rather than consume it.
