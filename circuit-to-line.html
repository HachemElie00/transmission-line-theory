Chapter 02

When a circuit becomes a line

Kirchhoff's laws are not laws. They are an approximation with a stated domain of validity, and that domain has a sharp and computable edge. Finding the edge tells you exactly when a piece of wire stops being a connection and starts being a component.

Assumes: chapter 01 — wavelength, phase constant, phasors.

The assumption nobody states

Kirchhoff's voltage law says the potentials around a loop sum to zero. Kirchhoff's current law says the currents into a node sum to zero. Both are used as though they hold at every instant — and buried in that word instant is the whole problem.

If you change the voltage at one end of a wire, when does the other end find out? Not immediately. The change propagates at the phase velocity of the medium, so it arrives after a delay

$$t_d = \frac{l}{u_p}$$ $l$ the physical length, $u_p$ the phase velocity — roughly $2\times10^8$ m/s in ordinary cable

Lumped circuit theory is what you get by declaring $t_d = 0$. Every node has one voltage, shared everywhere along it, at every moment. That is not a claim about wires being perfect; it is a claim about the delay being negligible compared with how fast the signal changes.

So the question is never "is this wire long?"

It is "is this delay short compared with one period?" A metre of cable is enormous at 10 GHz and invisible at 60 Hz. Length alone tells you nothing.

Electrical length

Compare the delay with the period of the signal and something clean happens. The delay is $t_d = l/u_p$, the period is $T = 1/f$, and their ratio is

$$\frac{t_d}{T} = \frac{l f}{u_p} = \frac{l}{\lambda}$$

The physical length and the velocity both cancel out. What survives is a single dimensionless number: how many wavelengths long the thing is. That number is the electrical length, and it is usually quoted as a phase:

$$\beta l = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\,l \quad\text{radians} \qquad\text{or}\qquad 360^\circ \times \frac{l}{\lambda}$$ the phase a wave accumulates crossing the thing — the only length that matters in this subject

From here on, lengths are quoted in wavelengths almost without exception. A "quarter-wave transformer" is not 3 cm long; it is $\lambda/4$ long, and what that is in centimetres depends on the frequency and the dielectric.

Drag the electrical length below and watch the voltage profile along one piece of wire. The wire occupies the same screen width throughout — only how much of a wavelength fits inside it changes.

At very small electrical length the profile is visibly flat: both ends really do sit at the same voltage, and calling it a single node costs nothing. As the electrical length grows the profile starts to tilt, then to curve, and at $\lambda/4$ the two ends can be at opposite polarity at the same instant. No amount of care with lumped components will reproduce that.

How much error, exactly

The two ends carry $\cos(\omega t)$ and $\cos(\omega t - \beta l)$. Their difference is itself a sinusoid, and a standard identity gives its amplitude:

$$\cos(\omega t) - \cos(\omega t - \beta l) = -2\sin\!\left(\omega t - \frac{\beta l}{2}\right)\sin\!\left(\frac{\beta l}{2}\right)$$ $$\text{peak discrepancy} = 2\left|\sin\frac{\beta l}{2}\right|$$ as a fraction of the signal amplitude — this is the error lumped theory commits by calling both ends one node

This is not a delay in the ordinary sense — every signal takes time to arrive somewhere, and nothing downstream is expecting a particular phase. The problem is narrower than that. Lumped theory assigns the whole wire a single node and a single voltage, true everywhere on it at every instant. The number above is how far the two ends actually disagree about what that one voltage is, at the same instant $t$. A node that disagrees with itself is not something any choice of lumped components can represent, which is exactly why a bigger discrepancy forces a different kind of model rather than just a less accurate lumped one.

Notice that the familiar $\lambda/10$ rule of thumb already tolerates a 62 % discrepancy between the ends of a supposedly equipotential node. It is a rule for rough work, not a guarantee. And notice there is no threshold anywhere in that table — nothing switches off, no regime changes. The error grows smoothly from nothing, and where you draw the line depends entirely on what you can tolerate.

Full derivation — putting real numbers on it

Take a signal at frequency $f$ on a line with phase velocity $u_p = c/\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\text{eff}}}$. The wavelength is $\lambda = u_p/f$, so a piece of hardware of length $l$ has electrical length

$$\frac{l}{\lambda} = \frac{l f \sqrt{\varepsilon_{\text{eff}}}}{c}$$

A 1 m mains cable at 60 Hz. With $u_p \approx 2\times10^8$ m/s, $\lambda = 2\times10^8/60 \approx 3300$ km. So $l/\lambda \approx 3\times10^{-7}$ and $\beta l \approx 0.0001^\circ$. Lumped theory is exact to more digits than you can measure.

A 2 cm board trace at 5 GHz, on FR-4 with $\varepsilon_{\text{eff}} \approx 3.5$. Then $u_p \approx 1.6\times10^8$ m/s, $\lambda \approx 3.2$ cm, and

$$\frac{l}{\lambda} = \frac{2}{3.2} = 0.63 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \beta l \approx 225^\circ$$

More than half a wavelength. That trace is not a connection between two components — it is a component, with its own impedance, and treating it as a wire will give an answer that is not merely imprecise but qualitatively wrong.

Same physical object, same equations, thirteen orders of magnitude between the two cases. The only thing that changed is $l/\lambda$.

Edges instead of frequencies

A digital signal has no single frequency, so "how many wavelengths long" needs a substitute. The edge supplies one. A step that rises in a time $t_r$ (10 % to 90 %) carries most of its energy below about

$$f_{\text{edge}} \approx \frac{0.35}{t_r} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{l}{\lambda_{\text{edge}}} = f_{\text{edge}}\,t_d \approx 0.35\,\frac{t_d}{t_r}$$ 0.35 is $2.2/2\pi$: a single-pole response with time constant $\tau$ rises in $2.2\tau$ and has its −3 dB point at $1/2\pi\tau$

So the comparison that matters is the line's delay against the edge, and the $\lambda/10$ rule becomes $t_r \gtrsim 3.5\,t_d$. Below that, the far end does not follow the source smoothly. It waits a full delay, then arrives in steps as the wave bounces between the mismatched ends.

With a slow edge the two models agree and the line is a capacitor. With a fast one the line model shows the staircase and the overshoot to 4/3 that the capacitor cannot produce at all. The overshoot is set by the two reflection coefficients, here $+1$ at the open end and $-1/3$ at the source, and chapter 03 draws the bounce diagram that produces it.

The way out

If the failure is that a single lumped element cannot represent something whose voltage varies along its length, there is an obvious repair: use more elements. Chop the line into short pieces, each short enough for lumped theory to hold inside it, and chain them.

Each little piece needs four things to represent what a real pair of conductors does:

The primes mark these as per unit length — ohms per metre, henries per metre, and so on — because a piece twice as long has twice as much of each. That single convention is what makes the model work for a line of any length.

Then take the pieces to be infinitesimally short and let calculus handle the limit. That is the next chapter, and the result is a pair of coupled differential equations that govern every transmission line there is.

What has actually changed

Nothing about the physics. Kirchhoff's laws are still used — they are simply applied over a length short enough for their assumption to hold, rather than over the whole cable at once. The distributed model is not a replacement for circuit theory; it is circuit theory applied honestly.

How finely to chop

The ladder is exact only in the limit, so it is worth knowing how quickly a finite one converges. Split a line of electrical length $\beta l$ into $N$ sections, each a series inductance $L'\Delta z$ between two shunt capacitances of $C'\Delta z/2$ (a $\pi$-section), terminate it, and compare its input impedance with the exact line's from chapter 08.

Two scalings are visible. For a fixed ladder the error grows roughly as the cube of the line's electrical length, and for a fixed line it falls as $1/N^2$: doubling the sections quarters it. With the 100 $\Omega$ load, one $\pi$-section is good to 1 % up to about $\lambda/17$ and eight up to about $\lambda/4$. This is the same convergence a circuit simulator relies on when it models a line as a ladder, and the reason it needs many sections per wavelength.

Where the line model itself stops

The distributed model fixes the lumped model's failure, but it has assumptions of its own, and both of them fail at high enough frequency.

Only one field pattern. The model assumes the fields in the cross-section have the static shape of part two's TEM solution. A coaxial cable can also carry higher-order modes, with field patterns that vary around and across the cross-section, once their cutoff is passed. The first, TE11, appears when the mean circumference is about one wavelength in the dielectric:

$$f_c \approx \frac{c}{\pi(a + b)\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}$$

For an SMA connector, with $a = 0.635$ mm, $b = 2.05$ mm and $\varepsilon_r = 2.05$, that is about 25 GHz, which is why the connector's specifications stop in the high teens to mid twenties of gigahertz. Above $f_c$ a second wave with its own $\beta$ and its own $Z_0$ shares the cable, and a single $V$ and $I$ no longer describe it.

No radiation. The model also assumes all the energy stays between the conductors. In coax it does, by construction. On an open two-wire line it does only while the spacing $s$ is small against $\lambda$: the currents in the two wires are equal and opposite, their fields cancel at a distance, but the cancellation is imperfect by an amount of order $s/\lambda$ and the radiated power grows as $(s/\lambda)^2$. Twin-lead is a good line at HF and a poor one at microwave frequencies, where the same structure starts to behave like an antenna.

Two scales, two tests

Lumped theory needs the length to be small against $\lambda$. The line model needs the cross-section to be small against $\lambda$. Every transmission line is long and thin in exactly this sense, and the whole subject lives in the gap between the two.

Self-check

Three questions on where the boundary really sits.

1. Two identical 10 cm cables carry different signals: one at 1 kHz, one at 3 GHz. Which needs transmission-line treatment?

At 1 kHz the wavelength is hundreds of kilometres, so 10 cm is around $10^{-6}\lambda$ and utterly lumped. At 3 GHz in cable the wavelength is roughly 7 cm, so the same 10 cm is over a full wavelength. Identical hardware, opposite verdicts — which is why the question is always $l/\lambda$ and never $l$.

2. What exactly does the lumped approximation assume?

Lumped models handle resistance perfectly well — that is what a resistor is. The assumption is zero propagation delay, which is what allows "a node" to have a single voltage. Once the delay is a noticeable fraction of a period, different points on the same node genuinely differ and no lumped element can express that.

3. Is there a frequency above which circuit theory stops working?

Nothing switches off at any frequency. The discrepancy $2|\sin(\beta l/2)|$ rises smoothly from zero, and the commonly quoted cutoffs of $\lambda/10$ or $\lambda/20$ are engineering conventions about acceptable error, not physical boundaries. A tiny circuit at 100 GHz can be perfectly lumped; a long one at 1 MHz may not be.
