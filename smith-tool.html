    Reading the outer scales

    The three rings around the chart are the part people skip, and they are the part that makes the chart usable without a calculator. From the inside out:

    RingWhat it readsWhere zero sits
    Angle of $\Gamma$the phase of the reflection coefficient, in degrees$0^\circ$ at the right-hand point (open circuit); $\pm180^\circ$ at the left (short)
    Wavelengths toward loaddistance travelled from the generator back toward the load$0$ at the left; increases counter-clockwise
    Wavelengths toward generatordistance travelled from the load toward the source$0$ at the left; increases clockwise

    Both wavelength rings run from $0$ to $0.5$ and then start again, because $\Gamma(d) = \Gamma_L e^{-j2\beta d}$ turns through a full $360^\circ$ every half wavelength. That is the same $\lambda/2$ repeat chapter 09 derived, printed on the rim.

    To use them the way you would on paper: find your load, read its position on the toward generator ring, add the distance you are travelling, and read the new position back off the same ring. The difference between two readings is a distance; the readings themselves are arbitrary until you subtract them.

    The one sign convention that matters

    Toward the generator is clockwise. Every arc on this page that walks along a line walks clockwise, and the two arrows in the corners of the chart say so. If a construction ever seems to come out half a wavelength wrong, this is almost always why.

    The scales under the chart

    Everything on the strip below the chart is a function of one number: $|\Gamma|$, the distance from the centre. That is why a real chart prints them as a ruler rather than as curves — measure the radius of your point with a compass, lay it on the ruler, and read all six at once.

    QuantityFrom $|\Gamma|$Reads
    SWR$\dfrac{1+|\Gamma|}{1-|\Gamma|}$the voltage ratio on the line
    SWR in dB$20\log_{10} S$the same thing, logarithmically
    Return loss$-20\log_{10}|\Gamma|$how far down the reflection is
    Mismatch loss$-10\log_{10}\!\left(1-|\Gamma|^2\right)$power lost to the reflection
    Reflected power$|\Gamma|^2$fraction of incident power turned back

    The transmission coefficient $\tau = 1 + \Gamma$ is not on the ruler, and the reason is worth saying out loud: it depends on the angle of $\Gamma$, not only its magnitude, so it is not a radial quantity at all. It is in the readout row instead, where it belongs.

    What the solver does

    Five methods. Each returns every solution rather than the first one found, lists each with its lengths in wavelengths and in millimetres at your frequency, and draws the circuit it produces underneath.

    Pozar's chapter on matching also covers binomial and Chebyshev multisection transformers and tapered lines. Those are not here, and not because they are hard: they are broadband designs built by choosing a set of section impedances from a polynomial, and there is nothing for the chart to construct. They belong with the algebra, not the geometry.

    Shunt stub

    Travel until the admittance is $y = 1 + jb$, then cancel $b$ with a stub in parallel. The constant-SWR circle crosses the $g = 1$ circle twice, so any load that is neither matched nor purely reactive has exactly two answers per half wavelength, and the second is often the more practical one.

    Series stub

    The same idea in impedance. Travel until $z = 1 + jx$, then cancel $x$ with a stub in series. Two solutions again, for the same reason. Series stubs are awkward in microstrip — you have to break the line — but natural in coax and in slotline.

    Double stub

    The single stub has a practical flaw: its position $d$ depends on the load, so the tuner has to be built again for every load. The double-stub tuner fixes the positions instead — stub 1 at the load, stub 2 a fixed distance $d$ away — and adjusts only the two lengths. That is what makes it a tuner rather than a one-off matching network.

    The construction turns on one fact: stub 1 changes $b$ but not $g$, so the point after it is still somewhere on the $g = g_L$ circle. The spacing then rotates that point, and it has to land on $g = 1$. So stub 1 must leave you on the $g = 1$ circle rotated back toward the load by $2\beta d$ — the dashed helper circle the tool draws. Where that circle cuts the $g = g_L$ circle, you have a solution; there are two crossings, so two designs.

    $$\operatorname{Re} y_2 = \frac{g\left(1+t^2\right)}{\left(1-tb\right)^2 + t^2 g^2}, \qquad t = \tan\beta d$$ setting this to 1 gives $\left(1-tb\right)^2 = g\left(1+t^2\right) - t^2g^2$, a quadratic in $b$

    The square root is real only when $g \le 1/\sin^2\beta d$, and that is the forbidden region — the shaded disc on the chart. A load inside it cannot be matched at that spacing by any pair of stub lengths, because stub 1 cannot move $g$ at all. The cures are to change the spacing, or to put a length of line in front of stub 1, which moves $g$ and drags the load out of the region. At $d = \lambda/8$ the limit is $g \le 2$; at $\lambda/4$ it tightens to $g \le 1$, which is why $\lambda/8$ and $3\lambda/8$ are the usual choices.

    Quarter-wave transformer

    A transformer needs a real impedance to work on, and a mismatched line offers exactly two per half wavelength: the voltage maximum, where $z = S$, and the voltage minimum, where $z = 1/S$. Put a $\lambda/4$ section of $Z_1 = \sqrt{Z_0 Z_\text{real}}$ at either one.

    L-network

    Two lumped reactances, no line length at all: one in series with the line and one across it, and which of the two sits next to the load depends on the load. The condition is geometric. A series element slides you along a constant-$r$ circle and a shunt element along a constant-$g$ circle, and the first element has to carry the load onto the circle the second can finish from. With the series element at the load that needs $r_L < 1$; with the shunt element at the load, $g_L < 1$. At least one always holds, and each order that works generally gives two solutions.

    Why methods sometimes come up empty

    The solver reports no solution rather than an approximate one. A matched load has nothing to match; a purely reactive load ($R = 0$) cannot be matched by any lossless network to a real $Z_0$, because it absorbs no power; and an L-network in one of its two orders is genuinely impossible for some loads. The message says which case you are in.

      Check yourself

      On the chart, you move from the load a distance of $0.125\lambda$ toward the generator. How far does the point rotate, and which way?

      The phase of $\Gamma$ advances by $2\beta d = 2(2\pi/\lambda)(0.125\lambda) = \pi/2$. A full turn is half a wavelength, so an eighth of a wavelength is a quarter turn — clockwise, because travelling toward the generator makes the phase more negative.

      Two students solve the same shunt-stub problem and get different answers, both of which the solver agrees are correct. What must be true?

      The constant-SWR circle cuts the $g = 1$ circle in two places, and either crossing gives a genuine match. They differ in stub position $d$, in the sign of the susceptance to be cancelled, and therefore in stub length — and usually in bandwidth too, which is the practical reason to compute both.

      A load reads $|\Gamma| = 0.5$. Roughly what is the return loss, and how much of the incident power comes back?

      $-20\log_{10}0.5 = 6.02$ dB, and the reflected power fraction is $|\Gamma|^2 = 0.25$. The factor-of-two trap is thinking in voltage where the question asks about power; the ruler under the chart lines both up so you can see them together.
