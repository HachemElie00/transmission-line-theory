Chapter 04

Propagation on a line

Two coupled equations, decoupled the same way the prologue decoupled the field laws. Out come the two numbers that describe any transmission line completely: how a wave changes as it travels, and the fixed ratio of voltage to current while it does.

Assumes: chapter 03 — the telegrapher's equations in phasor form.

Decoupling, again

Chapter 03 ended with two equations, each giving one quantity's slope in terms of the other:

$$-\frac{d\tilde{V}}{dz} = Z'\tilde{I} \qquad\qquad -\frac{d\tilde{I}}{dz} = Y'\tilde{V}$$

where $Z' = R' + j\omega L'$ and $Y' = G' + j\omega C'$. Neither can be solved alone, and the fix is the one from the prologue: differentiate one and substitute the other. Differentiating the first with respect to $z$ produces a $d\tilde{I}/dz$, which the second replaces — and $\tilde{I}$ leaves the problem.

$$\frac{d^2\tilde{V}}{dz^2} - \gamma^2\tilde{V} = 0 \qquad\text{with}\qquad \gamma^2 = Z'Y'$$ the same equation holds for $\tilde{I}$ — this is the Helmholtz equation of the prologue, in circuit variables
Full derivation — the substitution in full

Differentiate the first telegrapher's equation with respect to $z$. Both $R'$ and $L'$ are constants along a uniform line, so $Z'$ passes through the derivative:

$$-\frac{d^2\tilde{V}}{dz^2} = Z'\frac{d\tilde{I}}{dz}$$

The second equation gives $d\tilde{I}/dz = -Y'\tilde{V}$ directly. Substituting:

$$-\frac{d^2\tilde{V}}{dz^2} = -Z'Y'\tilde{V} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d^2\tilde{V}}{dz^2} = Z'Y'\,\tilde{V}$$

Doing the same in the other order — differentiate the second, substitute the first — gives the identical equation for $\tilde{I}$. That symmetry matters: voltage and current obey the same differential equation, so they have the same propagation behaviour and differ only in amplitude and sign.

Note what was assumed: that $R'$, $L'$, $G'$ and $C'$ do not vary with $z$. A tapered line, or one passing between two dielectrics, breaks that and the derivative of $Z'$ contributes an extra term.

A second-order equation of that form has exponential solutions, and there are two of them:

$$\tilde{V}(z) = V_0^+ e^{-\gamma z} + V_0^- e^{+\gamma z}$$ one decaying toward $+z$, one toward $-z$ — a forward wave and a backward wave

Everything in the remaining chapters is a consequence of those two terms and what happens where they meet a boundary. There is no third solution: a uniform line supports exactly one wave in each direction, and nothing else.

The propagation constant

$\gamma$ is complex, and its two parts do separate jobs. Writing $\gamma = \alpha + j\beta$ and expanding the forward term:

$$e^{-\gamma z} = e^{-\alpha z}\,e^{-j\beta z}$$ a shrinking magnitude times a turning phase — the two effects are completely separable
PartNameUnitsDoes
$\alpha$attenuation constantNp/mshrinks the amplitude: $e^{-\alpha z}$
$\beta$phase constantrad/madvances the phase: $e^{-j\beta z}$

And $\gamma$ itself comes straight from the four line parameters:

$$\gamma = \sqrt{Z'Y'} = \sqrt{(R' + j\omega L')(G' + j\omega C')}$$ compare the prologue's $\gamma = \sqrt{j\omega\mu(\sigma + j\omega\varepsilon)}$ — identical structure, circuit quantities in place of material ones

That resemblance is not decorative. The circuit model was built to describe the same physics the field equations describe, so the two expressions had to agree — and $\alpha$, $\beta$ mean exactly what they meant there.

The attenuation unit deserves a note. A neper is a natural-log unit: $\alpha = 1$ Np/m means the amplitude falls by a factor $e$ every metre. Datasheets quote decibels instead, and the conversion is $1\ \text{Np} = 8.686\ \text{dB}$.

Characteristic impedance

The wave equation gave the voltage. The current follows by putting that solution back into the telegrapher's equation it came from, and the result is remarkably tidy:

$$\tilde{I}(z) = \frac{V_0^+}{Z_0}e^{-\gamma z} - \frac{V_0^-}{Z_0}e^{+\gamma z} \qquad\text{with}\qquad Z_0 = \frac{Z'}{\gamma} = \sqrt{\frac{R' + j\omega L'}{G' + j\omega C'}}$$ note the minus sign on the backward term — the same sign that made reflection possible in the prologue
Full derivation — and why $Z_0$ falls out as a constant

Take the voltage solution and differentiate it:

$$\frac{d\tilde{V}}{dz} = -\gamma V_0^+ e^{-\gamma z} + \gamma V_0^- e^{+\gamma z}$$

The first telegrapher's equation says this equals $-Z'\tilde{I}$, so

$$\tilde{I}(z) = \frac{\gamma}{Z'}\left(V_0^+ e^{-\gamma z} - V_0^- e^{+\gamma z}\right)$$

The factor $\gamma/Z'$ has units of admittance and does not depend on $z$ at all. Defining $Z_0 = Z'/\gamma$ gives the boxed result. Expanding it with $\gamma = \sqrt{Z'Y'}$:

$$Z_0 = \frac{Z'}{\sqrt{Z'Y'}} = \sqrt{\frac{Z'}{Y'}} = \sqrt{\frac{R'+j\omega L'}{G'+j\omega C'}}$$

Where the minus sign comes from. It is not a convention. The backward voltage wave carries $e^{+\gamma z}$, so differentiating it produces $+\gamma$ rather than $-\gamma$, and that sign survives into the current. Physically: a wave travelling in $-z$ carries power in $-z$, which requires the current to reverse relative to its voltage. Take the ratio for each wave separately and

$$\frac{\tilde{V}^+}{\tilde{I}^+} = +Z_0 \qquad\qquad \frac{\tilde{V}^-}{\tilde{I}^-} = -Z_0$$

That asymmetry is the whole reason a mismatched load can be satisfied at all, and chapter 06 uses it to derive $\Gamma$ in two lines.

What $Z_0$ is not

It is not a resistance you could find with an ohmmeter, and no power is dissipated in it. It is the ratio of voltage to current within a single travelling wave — the same kind of object as the intrinsic impedance $\eta = \sqrt{\mu/\varepsilon}$ from the prologue, and for the same reason. A 50 Ω cable measures as a short or an open at DC depending on what is on the far end.

One more caution. $Z_0$ relates $\tilde{V}^+$ to $\tilde{I}^+$ for one wave. On a line carrying both waves, the total voltage divided by the total current is not $Z_0$ — it varies with position, and working out what it is instead is the subject of chapter 08.

The lossless line

Set $R' = G' = 0$ and everything simplifies at once:

$$\gamma = \sqrt{(j\omega L')(j\omega C')} = j\omega\sqrt{L'C'}$$ $$\alpha = 0 \qquad \beta = \omega\sqrt{L'C'} \qquad Z_0 = \sqrt{\frac{L'}{C'}}$$ $\gamma$ is purely imaginary, so nothing decays; $Z_0$ is purely real, so voltage and current stay in phase

The derived quantities follow immediately:

QuantityLossless valueRemark
Phase velocity$u_p = \dfrac{\omega}{\beta} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{L'C'}}$independent of frequency — no dispersion
Wavelength$\lambda = \dfrac{2\pi}{\beta} = \dfrac{u_p}{f}$as in chapter 01
$Z_0$$\sqrt{L'/C'}$real, frequency-independent

That $u_p$ does not depend on frequency is worth pausing on. Every frequency component travels at the same speed, so a pulse made of many components keeps its shape. A lossy line generally does not have this property, and the resulting spreading — dispersion — is what limits data rates on long cables.

The bridge back to the fields

For any TEM line, $L'C' = \mu\varepsilon$ exactly. So $u_p = 1/\sqrt{L'C'} = 1/\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon}$ — the prologue's wave speed, recovered from circuit parameters. The geometry cancels out completely: the speed on a line is set by the dielectric alone, not by the conductor spacing. Only $Z_0$ depends on the geometry.

Seeing the parameters move

Everything above is one square root of a ratio of two complex numbers. Change the four line parameters and watch what happens to $\gamma$, $Z_0$ and the wave itself.

The window is a fixed metre of line, so it never rescales under you.

The last two presets are the ones worth understanding, because together they separate the two things $L'$ and $C'$ control. $Z_0$ depends on their ratio; $\lambda$ depends on their product. So you can move one while holding the other fixed:

That second preset is the important one, and it is the reason $Z_0$ is printed on the figure. Nothing about the drawn wave changes when you switch to it — same wavelength, same envelope, same everything — yet the line's impedance has doubled. $Z_0$ is invisible in a picture of $v(z)$ alone; it only reveals itself in the ratio of voltage to current, or in what happens when the wave meets a load.

One honest note on the loss presets. Pick real coax and the envelope looks flat — because it is. Good cable loses well under a percent over a metre; you need hundreds of metres before attenuation is visible at this scale. The exaggerated loss preset uses values no real cable has, purely so that $\alpha$ does something you can see.

Why 50 Ω is a high-frequency statement

$Z_0 = \sqrt{L'/C'}$ holds when $\omega L' \gg R'$ and $\omega C' \gg G'$ — that is, when the reactive terms dominate the lossy ones. At low frequency they do not, and the same cable behaves entirely differently.

At the bottom of that sweep $Z_0 \to \sqrt{R'/G'}$, a few hundred ohms and purely real — the line behaves as a resistive divider, not as a wave medium. Through the middle it is complex, and for this cable $\angle Z_0$ bottoms out near −36° around 10 kHz. Only above roughly a megahertz does it settle onto the flat, real 50 Ω that the cable is sold as.

That −36° is worth one more line, because it is not an arbitrary number and it is not the worst case either. Since $\angle Z_0 = \tfrac12\left(\angle Z' - \angle Y'\right)$, and both $\angle Z'$ and $\angle Y'$ are confined between $0$ and $90^\circ$ for any passive line, $|\angle Z_0|$ can never exceed 45°. That bound is approached only when the two corner frequencies $R'/L'$ and $G'/C'$ are infinitely far apart; here they differ by a factor of 40, which gets you to 36° and no further.

So "RG-58 is 50 Ω" is shorthand for "well above its transition frequency, this cable's $\sqrt{L'/C'}$ is 50 Ω". For the RF work in the rest of this site that is always true, and $Z_0$ can be treated as a real constant — but it is an approximation with a domain, like every other one in this subject.

Full derivation — the low-loss approximation

Real cable is not lossless, but at RF it is usually low-loss: $R' \ll \omega L'$ and $G' \ll \omega C'$. Factor those small quantities out of $\gamma$:

$$\gamma = j\omega\sqrt{L'C'}\sqrt{\left(1 + \frac{R'}{j\omega L'}\right)\left(1 + \frac{G'}{j\omega C'}\right)}$$

Each square root is of the form $\sqrt{1+x}$ with $|x| \ll 1$, so expand to first order using $\sqrt{1+x} \approx 1 + x/2$ and keep only first-order terms:

$$\gamma \approx j\omega\sqrt{L'C'}\left(1 + \frac{R'}{2j\omega L'} + \frac{G'}{2j\omega C'}\right)$$

Separating real and imaginary parts gives the standard results:

$$\alpha \approx \frac{R'}{2}\sqrt{\frac{C'}{L'}} + \frac{G'}{2}\sqrt{\frac{L'}{C'}} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{R'}{Z_0} + G'Z_0\right)$$
$$\beta \approx \omega\sqrt{L'C'} \qquad\qquad Z_0 \approx \sqrt{\frac{L'}{C'}}$$

Three things to take from this. The phase constant is unchanged to first order, so a low-loss line still has essentially no dispersion. The attenuation splits cleanly into a conductor term and a dielectric term, which is why datasheets plot two loss mechanisms. And $Z_0$ returns to its real lossless value, which is why treating it as a real number is defensible at RF and indefensible at audio.

A lossy line that does not distort

Loss alone does not spread a pulse. What spreads it is loss and phase velocity that depend on frequency, so that the pulse's components arrive at different strengths and different times. Heaviside noticed that one special balance between the four parameters removes the frequency dependence entirely, loss and all:

$$\frac{R'}{L'} = \frac{G'}{C'} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \alpha = \sqrt{R'G'}, \qquad \beta = \omega\sqrt{L'C'}, \qquad Z_0 = \sqrt{\frac{L'}{C'}}$$ exactly, at every frequency: the line attenuates every component equally and delays every component equally
Full derivation — why the balance makes everything frequency-independent

Call the common ratio $k = R'/L' = G'/C'$. Then both per-unit-length quantities share a factor:

$$Z' = R' + j\omega L' = L'(k + j\omega), \qquad Y' = G' + j\omega C' = C'(k + j\omega)$$

so the square roots come out exactly, with no approximation:

$$\gamma = \sqrt{Z'Y'} = \sqrt{L'C'}\,(k + j\omega), \qquad Z_0 = \sqrt{\frac{Z'}{Y'}} = \sqrt{\frac{L'}{C'}}$$

The real part of $\gamma$ is $k\sqrt{L'C'} = \sqrt{R'G'}$, independent of $\omega$; the imaginary part is $\omega\sqrt{L'C'}$, proportional to $\omega$, so $u_p = 1/\sqrt{L'C'}$ for every component. A signal arrives smaller by $e^{-\alpha z}$ and later by $z\sqrt{L'C'}$, and otherwise unchanged.

With $G' = 0$ the low frequencies are hardly attenuated while the high ones are, so the pulse loses its sharp parts and smears into a long tail: this is what limited the first undersea telegraph cables to a few words a minute. A real cable has $G'/C'$ far smaller than $R'/L'$, and Heaviside's remedy was to raise $L'$, not $G'$, since adding leakage wastes signal. Loading coils spaced about every two kilometres did exactly that on telephone lines for most of the twentieth century.

Where the loss comes from, across frequency

The low-loss result splits the attenuation into a part from each lossy parameter:

$$\alpha = \alpha_c + \alpha_d, \qquad \alpha_c = \frac{R'}{2Z_0} \propto \sqrt{f}, \qquad \alpha_d = \frac{G'Z_0}{2} = \frac{\beta}{2}\tan\delta \propto f$$ conductor loss grows as the square root of frequency, dielectric loss in proportion to it; power falls as $e^{-2\alpha z}$

On a good coax dielectric the conductor dominates through the whole microwave range, and loss per metre roughly doubles for every factor of four in frequency. On FR-4 the dielectric takes over from a few tens of megahertz upward, its loss rising in proportion to frequency, which is why high-speed board design moves to lower-loss laminates long before the copper becomes the problem. In both cases a datasheet's dB per 100 m is this sum, measured.

Self-check

Three questions on what these two constants mean.

1. You measure a 50 Ω coax with an ohmmeter, far end open. What do you read?

$Z_0$ is the ratio of voltage to current in a travelling wave, and at DC there are no waves. An ohmmeter sees only the series resistance of the conductors and the leakage through the dielectric, so an open-ended cable reads open and a shorted one reads near zero. Nothing about either measurement contradicts the cable being 50 Ω at RF.

2. You double $L'$ and halve $C'$. What happens to $Z_0$ and to $u_p$?

$Z_0 = \sqrt{L'/C'}$ depends on the ratio, which goes up by four, so $Z_0$ doubles. $u_p = 1/\sqrt{L'C'}$ depends on the product, and doubling one while halving the other leaves it untouched. The first and second options say the same thing — the second states the reason, which is the part worth carrying.

3. On a line carrying both a forward and a backward wave, is the ratio of total voltage to total current equal to $Z_0$?

The forward wave has $\tilde V^+/\tilde I^+ = +Z_0$ and the backward wave has $\tilde V^-/\tilde I^- = -Z_0$. Because of that sign, the totals do not combine into $Z_0$ — the ratio varies with position along the line. That varying ratio is exactly the input impedance of chapter 08, and it equals $Z_0$ everywhere only when the backward wave is absent.
