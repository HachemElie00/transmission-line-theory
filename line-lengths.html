Chapter 09

Line lengths and transformers

Two particular lengths do something useful enough to have names. A half wavelength changes nothing at all; a quarter wavelength turns an impedance upside down. From that second fact comes the simplest matching network there is, and from stubs of controlled length comes any reactance you like.

Assumes: chapter 08 — the input impedance formula.

The half-wave repeat

Put $d = \lambda/2$ into the tangent formula. Then $\beta d = \pi$, and $\tan\pi = 0$, so both tangent terms vanish:

$$Z_{\text{in}}\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}\right) = Z_0\,\frac{Z_L + jZ_0\cdot 0}{Z_0 + jZ_L\cdot 0} = Z_L$$ a half-wave line is electrically invisible — whatever the load is, that is what you see

And since the same argument applies to any whole number of half wavelengths, adding $n\lambda/2$ to a line changes nothing. This is the same fact as the $\Gamma$ rotation completing a full lap, seen from the impedance side.

It has a practical edge worth passing on. A half-wave line repeats the load at the design frequency only. Move off that frequency and the electrical length is no longer exactly $\pi$, so the repeat fails — and it fails faster the more half-waves you stacked up. A long cable is a narrowband component even when it looks like a plain connection.

The quarter-wave inverter

Now put $d = \lambda/4$, so $\beta d = \pi/2$ and $\tan\beta d \to \infty$. Divide numerator and denominator by the tangent before taking the limit and the finite terms drop out:

$$Z_{\text{in}}\left(\frac{\lambda}{4}\right) = Z_0\,\frac{Z_L/\tan\beta d + jZ_0}{Z_0/\tan\beta d + jZ_L} \;\longrightarrow\; Z_0\,\frac{jZ_0}{jZ_L} = \frac{Z_0^2}{Z_L}$$ the load is inverted about $Z_0$ — large becomes small, small becomes large

In normalised form, where $z = Z/Z_0$, it is cleaner still:

$$z_{\text{in}} = \frac{1}{z_L}$$

A quarter-wave line turns a normalised impedance into its reciprocal — which is to say, it turns impedance into admittance. That single sentence is why the Smith chart has an admittance overlay, and why it is the same chart rotated half a turn. Chapter 10 makes that explicit.

The quarter-wave transformer

Turn the inverter around and it becomes a matching network. Suppose a real load $R_L$ must be matched to a line of impedance $Z_0$. Insert a quarter-wave section of some other impedance $Z_1$ between them. Looking into that section:

$$Z_{\text{in}} = \frac{Z_1^2}{R_L}$$

For a match we need $Z_{\text{in}} = Z_0$, which fixes $Z_1$ immediately:

$$Z_1 = \sqrt{Z_0 R_L}$$ the geometric mean of what you have and what you want

That is the whole design. One section, one formula, no iteration — and it is exact at the design frequency.

The figure shows the catch. $|\Gamma|$ is exactly zero at $f_0$ and climbs on both sides, because away from the design frequency the section is no longer a quarter wavelength.

Bandwidth is not a property of the transformer — it is a property of the transformer plus your tolerance. Since the match degrades continuously, there is no natural edge to the band; you have to declare how much reflection you are willing to live with. That is what the second slider sets. Put it at $|\Gamma| = 0.2$ and you are saying "anything under 4 % reflected power, an SWR of 1.5, counts as matched" — and the shaded stripe is then the frequency range that qualifies.

With the limit fixed, push $R_L$ away from $Z_0$ and the stripe narrows: a harder match is a narrower one. Compare the 100 Ω preset with the 200 Ω one. That trade-off is fundamental rather than a defect of this particular network, and beating it needs multiple sections or a tapered line.

Full derivation — where the bandwidth number comes from

At a frequency $f$, the section still has its physical length $\lambda_0/4$, but the electrical length has changed to

$$\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}\cdot\frac{f}{f_0}$$

Using the cosine form of the input impedance avoids the singularity at $\theta = \pi/2$:

$$Z_{\text{in}} = Z_1\,\frac{R_L\cos\theta + jZ_1\sin\theta}{Z_1\cos\theta + jR_L\sin\theta}$$

and then $\Gamma = (Z_{\text{in}} - Z_0)/(Z_{\text{in}} + Z_0)$ as always. Working the algebra through gives a compact closed form:

$$|\Gamma| = \frac{\left|R_L - Z_0\right|\left|\cos\theta\right|}{\sqrt{(R_L+Z_0)^2\cos^2\theta + 4Z_0R_L\sin^2\theta}}$$

At $\theta = \pi/2$ the cosine vanishes and so does $|\Gamma|$, confirming the exact match. Setting $|\Gamma| = \Gamma_m$ and solving for $\theta$ gives the band edges, and the fractional bandwidth is

$$\frac{\Delta f}{f_0} = 2 - \frac{4}{\pi}\cos^{-1}\left[\frac{\Gamma_m}{\sqrt{1-\Gamma_m^2}}\cdot\frac{2\sqrt{Z_0R_L}}{\left|R_L - Z_0\right|}\right]$$

The structure is what matters more than the formula. The mismatch $|R_L - Z_0|$ sits in the denominator inside the arccosine, so a bigger mismatch shrinks the bandwidth. The readout above evaluates this expression directly, and the match-limit slider sets $\Gamma_m$.

More sections, more bandwidth

One quarter-wave section matches perfectly at one frequency. Several, each stepping the impedance part of the way, can hold a good match over a wide band. The design rests on one approximation: when every step is small, each junction reflects a little and the reflections barely interact, so the total is simply their sum, each delayed by its round trip:

$$\Gamma(\theta) \approx \Gamma_0 + \Gamma_1e^{-j2\theta} + \Gamma_2e^{-j4\theta} + \cdots + \Gamma_Ne^{-j2N\theta}, \qquad \Gamma_n = \frac{Z_{n+1} - Z_n}{Z_{n+1} + Z_n} \approx \tfrac12\ln\frac{Z_{n+1}}{Z_n}$$ the theory of small reflections; $\theta = \beta l$ is each section's electrical length, $\pi/2$ at the design frequency

That is a polynomial in $e^{-j2\theta}$, and choosing the $\Gamma_n$ is choosing its shape. Two choices are standard.

Binomial makes $\Gamma(\theta)$ as flat as possible at the centre: all its first $N-1$ derivatives vanish there. The coefficients are binomial coefficients, $\Gamma_n \propto \binom{N}{n}$, so $\ln Z_{n+1} - \ln Z_n = 2^{-N}\binom{N}{n}\ln(Z_L/Z_0)$.

Chebyshev instead lets $|\Gamma|$ ripple up to a chosen ceiling $\Gamma_m$ across the band, which buys considerably more bandwidth for the same number of sections. The response is a Chebyshev polynomial, which has exactly that equal-ripple property:

$$\Gamma(\theta) = \Gamma_m\,e^{-jN\theta}\,T_N\!\left(\sec\theta_m\cos\theta\right), \qquad \sec\theta_m = \cosh\!\left[\frac1N\cosh^{-1}\frac{|\Gamma(0)|}{\Gamma_m}\right]$$

Three things show. The design formula and the exact response agree closely, which is what justifies designing with the approximation. More sections widen the band, and Chebyshev's equal ripple uses the allowance better than binomial's flatness. And an exponential taper, the limit of infinitely many infinitesimal steps with $\ln Z$ rising linearly, behaves like a high-pass match: poor at low frequency where it is short in wavelengths, and good above, its reflection falling as $|\sin\beta L/\beta L|$. Klopfenstein's taper refines it into the continuous counterpart of Chebyshev, the shortest taper for a given ripple.

Full derivation — the sum of small reflections

At each junction a wave arriving from the left is partly reflected, by $\Gamma_n$, and partly transmitted, by $1 + \Gamma_n$. The first-order reflection from junction $n$ has crossed $n$ sections twice, picking up $e^{-j2n\theta}$, and every transmission factor on the way is $1 + O(\Gamma)$. Keeping only terms first order in the $\Gamma$'s, and so ignoring the waves that bounce more than once inside the transformer, every junction's contribution simply adds. Second-order terms are products of two small reflections; with steps of a few percent they are a few tenths of a percent, which the exact curve above confirms.

For a continuous taper the sum becomes an integral over the line, with the local reflection per unit length $\tfrac12\,d(\ln Z)/dz$:

$$\Gamma(\theta) = \frac12\int_0^L e^{-j2\beta z}\,\frac{d}{dz}\ln\frac{Z(z)}{Z_0}\,dz$$

which for the exponential taper, $d(\ln Z)/dz$ constant, integrates to $\tfrac12\ln(Z_L/Z_0)\,e^{-j\beta L}\sin(\beta L)/\beta L$.

Stubs: reactance to order

Chapter 08 gave the input impedance of a shorted and an open line. Both are purely imaginary, and both sweep every value as the length changes:

$$Z_{\text{short}} = jZ_0\tan\beta l \qquad\qquad Z_{\text{open}} = -jZ_0\cot\beta l$$ no resistance at all on a lossless line — a stub stores energy, it does not dissipate it

That makes a length of line a tunable reactive component. Need a particular inductance at 5 GHz? Cut a shorted stub to the length whose tangent gives it. At microwave frequencies this is often easier, cheaper and far more repeatable than sourcing a lumped part — and it is why boards at those frequencies are covered in little rectangular stubs.

Read down the two columns and the pattern is clear: the open stub is the shorted stub shifted by a quarter wavelength, which is the inverter result again. Anything one can do, the other can do at a different length — so the choice between them is practical. A shorted stub needs a via to ground, which costs manufacturing effort; an open stub radiates a little from its exposed end. Below a few GHz, open stubs are usually easier.

Where this is going

A stub can supply any reactance. Chapter 08 showed the line can turn any load into a point with the right resistance. Put those together — slide along the line until the real part is correct, then cancel the imaginary part with a stub — and you have single-stub matching. That is chapter 11, and the Smith chart of chapter 10 is what makes it a two-minute construction instead of an algebra problem.

Stubs as lumped components: Richards and Kuroda

A shorted stub has $X = Z_0\tan\beta l$; a lumped inductor has $X = \omega L$. Richards noticed that these become the same function under one change of frequency variable, $\Omega = \tan\beta l$. Fix every stub at $\lambda/8$ long at a chosen frequency $f_c$, so that $\Omega = 1$ there, and

$$jX_L = j\Omega L \;\longleftrightarrow\; \text{shorted stub of } Z_0 = L \qquad\qquad jB_C = j\Omega C \;\longleftrightarrow\; \text{open stub of } Y_0 = C$$ Richards' transformation: any lumped LC filter becomes a stub filter with the same response, with $\omega/\omega_c$ replaced by $\tan(\pi f/4f_c)$

Below $f_c$ the two filters are indistinguishable. Above it the mapping shows: $\tan$ races to infinity at $2f_c$, so the stub filter has an infinitely deep stop at twice the cutoff, then repeats its whole response every $4f_c$. A distributed filter is always periodic in frequency, which is the price of building it from lengths.

One practical problem remains. Richards' transformation turns a series inductor into a series stub, which microstrip cannot build without breaking the line. Kuroda's identities fix that. Add a unit element, a plain length of line $\lambda/8$ long at $f_c$ like the stubs, and a series stub on one side of it can be traded for a shunt stub on the other. One of the four identities reads

$$\text{UE}(Z_1)\;\to\;\text{shunt open stub}(Z_2) \;\equiv\; \text{series shorted stub}\!\left(\frac{Z_1}{n^2}\right)\;\to\;\text{UE}\!\left(\frac{Z_2}{n^2}\right), \qquad n^2 = 1 + \frac{Z_2}{Z_1}$$

Read right to left, it turns a series stub into a shunt one. Applied stage by stage, the identities move every series stub to a shunt position and leave a filter of shunt stubs separated by unit elements, which is a printable layout. The unit elements also physically separate the stubs, which the lumped prototype never had to do.

A quarter wave on the board

Every length in this chapter is in guide wavelengths, $\lambda_g = \lambda_0/\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\text{eff}}}$, measured along the line itself. On microstrip that brings in chapter 05's two corrections. $\varepsilon_{\text{eff}}$ depends on the track's width, so a quarter-wave transformer section, narrower or wider than the line it matches, has a different $\lambda_g$ and must be cut to its own length rather than the main line's. And dispersion raises $\varepsilon_{\text{eff}}$ with frequency, so the section is electrically slightly longer above $f_0$ than a non-dispersive model predicts, which shifts and tilts the band in the figures above.

Self-check

Three questions on using length as a component.

1. A 75 Ω antenna must be matched to a 50 Ω line. What impedance quarter-wave section is needed?

$Z_1 = \sqrt{Z_0 R_L} = \sqrt{3750} = 61.2\ \Omega$. The geometric mean, not the arithmetic one — because the section inverts through $Z_1^2$, not through addition. The two means are close for a mild mismatch like this, which is exactly why the error is easy to make and hard to spot.

2. Why does a shorted quarter-wave stub look like an open circuit?

It is the inversion, applied to $Z_L = 0$. Equivalently: the short forces a voltage node at its end, and a quarter wavelength away sits the voltage antinode — maximum voltage, minimum current, which is what an open circuit looks like. Both descriptions are the same fact. And it still conducts DC, which is why the trick is so useful for bias feeds.

3. Two quarter-wave transformers, one matching 55 Ω and one matching 200 Ω, both to 50 Ω. Which has wider bandwidth?

Both are perfect at $f_0$, but bandwidth depends on how quickly $|\Gamma|$ rises either side. The mismatch $|R_L - Z_0|$ appears in the bandwidth expression such that a bigger mismatch narrows the band. 55 Ω is nearly matched already and stays acceptable over a wide range; 200 Ω needs the transformer to work hard and only does so near $f_0$.
