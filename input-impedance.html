Chapter 08

Input impedance

A generator driving a line does not see the load. It sees the load through the line, and what arrives at its terminals depends on how far away the load is. This is the chapter where a length of cable stops being a connection and becomes a component in its own right.

Assumes: chapter 06 — $\Gamma$ and its rotation; chapter 07 for the standing-wave connection.

What the generator sees

Chapter 04 warned that on a line carrying two waves, the ratio of total voltage to total current is not $Z_0$. Now we work out what it actually is. Standing a distance $d$ back from the load:

$$\tilde{V}(d) = V_0^+\left(e^{j\beta d} + \Gamma e^{-j\beta d}\right) \qquad \tilde{I}(d) = \frac{V_0^+}{Z_0}\left(e^{j\beta d} - \Gamma e^{-j\beta d}\right)$$

Their ratio is the input impedance at that point: whatever a source connected there would measure.

$$Z_{\text{in}}(d) = \frac{\tilde{V}(d)}{\tilde{I}(d)} = Z_0\,\frac{1 + \Gamma e^{-j2\beta d}}{1 - \Gamma e^{-j2\beta d}} = Z_0\,\frac{1 + \Gamma(d)}{1 - \Gamma(d)}$$ the same map from $\Gamma$ to impedance as at the load — only now $\Gamma$ has rotated

That form is worth pausing on, because it says something structural. The relationship between $\Gamma$ and impedance is always the same function. What changes along the line is not the rule, only the value of $\Gamma$ being fed into it — and chapter 06 already told us how that value moves: same magnitude, angle turning at $2\beta$.

Two facts you already have, combined

$\Gamma$ rotates on a circle of fixed radius, one lap per $\lambda/2$. Impedance is a fixed function of $\Gamma$. Therefore $Z_{\text{in}}$ must also repeat every half wavelength, and must trace a closed curve. That is the entire behaviour of this chapter, derived without touching a trigonometric identity.

The standard form

Substituting $\Gamma = (Z_L - Z_0)/(Z_L + Z_0)$ and grinding through the exponentials gives the expression you will actually use:

$$Z_{\text{in}}(d) = Z_0\,\frac{Z_L + jZ_0\tan\beta d}{Z_0 + jZ_L\tan\beta d}$$ lossless line; $\beta d$ is the electrical length of chapter 02, in radians
Full derivation — from exponentials to the tangent

Start from the ratio and substitute $\Gamma$:

$$Z_{\text{in}} = Z_0\,\frac{(Z_L+Z_0)e^{j\beta d} + (Z_L-Z_0)e^{-j\beta d}}{(Z_L+Z_0)e^{j\beta d} - (Z_L-Z_0)e^{-j\beta d}}$$

Expand the products and gather the $Z_L$ terms separately from the $Z_0$ terms. In the numerator:

$$Z_L\left(e^{j\beta d} + e^{-j\beta d}\right) + Z_0\left(e^{j\beta d} - e^{-j\beta d}\right)$$

Those two brackets are exactly the Euler forms $2\cos\beta d$ and $2j\sin\beta d$. The denominator gathers the other way round. So

$$Z_{\text{in}} = Z_0\,\frac{2Z_L\cos\beta d + 2jZ_0\sin\beta d}{2Z_0\cos\beta d + 2jZ_L\sin\beta d}$$

Cancel the twos and divide top and bottom by $\cos\beta d$:

$$Z_{\text{in}} = Z_0\,\frac{Z_L + jZ_0\tan\beta d}{Z_0 + jZ_L\tan\beta d}$$

The tangent is where the $\lambda/2$ periodicity becomes visible, since $\tan$ has period $\pi$ and $\beta d = \pi$ means $d = \lambda/2$. It also explains the singularities: at $\beta d = \pi/2$ the tangent blows up, and the expression collapses to $Z_0^2/Z_L$ — the quarter-wave result of the next chapter.

For a lossy line the same derivation with $\gamma$ in place of $j\beta$ replaces every tangent with a hyperbolic one:

$$Z_{\text{in}} = Z_0\,\frac{Z_L + Z_0\tanh\gamma d}{Z_0 + Z_L\tanh\gamma d}$$

Everything you need follows by putting particular values into that one expression.

Case$Z_{\text{in}}$Meaning

The first row deserves emphasis because it is the goal of all matching work. When $Z_L = Z_0$ there is no reflected wave, $\Gamma$ is zero and has nothing to rotate, so the line's length stops mattering entirely. That is the only condition under which a cable behaves like the ideal wire of first-year circuits.

Watching it rotate

Set a load and walk back along the line. The left panel is the $\Gamma$ plane from chapter 06; the right is $Z_{\text{in}}$ resolved into resistance and reactance as you move.

Three things to notice while sweeping. The $\Gamma$ dot travels clockwise and returns to its start after $d = \lambda/2$, which is why the traces on the right are periodic with that period, not $\lambda$. The resistance and reactance both swing wildly even though the load is fixed — a 25 Ω resistor seen through a line can look like almost anything. And the trace crosses the real axis twice per half wavelength: those two crossings are the voltage minimum and maximum of chapter 07.

Why the minimum trick works

Chapter 07 offered a check without proof: at a voltage minimum the impedance looking toward the load is purely real and equals $Z_0/S$. That falls out immediately now.

A voltage minimum is where $\Gamma(d)$ points along the negative real axis, so $\Gamma(d) = -|\Gamma|$. Substituting into the boxed expression:

$$Z_{\text{in}}\big|_{\min} = Z_0\,\frac{1 - |\Gamma|}{1 + |\Gamma|} = \frac{Z_0}{S}$$ real, because $\Gamma$ there is real and negative

And at a maximum $\Gamma(d) = +|\Gamma|$, giving the mirror result:

$$Z_{\text{in}}\big|_{\max} = Z_0\,\frac{1 + |\Gamma|}{1 - |\Gamma|} = S\,Z_0$$

So the standing-wave extrema are not merely places where the envelope peaks — they are the two points per half wavelength where the line looks like a pure resistance. That is why slotted-line measurements target them, and it is the fact every stub-matching procedure in chapter 11 is built on.

A bridge worth making explicit

$S$, $|\Gamma|$ and $Z_{\text{in}}$ at the extrema are three names for one quantity. Measure any of them and you have the other two: $S = Z_0/Z_{\min} = (1+|\Gamma|)/(1-|\Gamma|)$.

Folding the line into a circuit

The practical payoff of $Z_{\text{in}}$ is that it lets you delete the line. A generator of open-circuit voltage $\tilde{V}_g$ and internal impedance $Z_g$, driving a length of line into a load, becomes an ordinary two-element divider:

$$\tilde{V}_{\text{in}} = \tilde{V}_g\,\frac{Z_{\text{in}}}{Z_g + Z_{\text{in}}} \qquad\qquad \tilde{I}_{\text{in}} = \frac{\tilde{V}_g}{Z_g + Z_{\text{in}}}$$ ordinary lumped circuit analysis, valid because $Z_{\text{in}}$ already contains the whole line

That gives conditions at the input. To get back to the wave amplitudes — and hence to conditions anywhere along the line — evaluate the general solution at $z = -l$ and solve for $V_0^+$:

$$\tilde{V}_{\text{in}} = V_0^+\left(e^{j\beta l} + \Gamma e^{-j\beta l}\right) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad V_0^+ = \frac{\tilde{V}_{\text{in}}}{e^{j\beta l} + \Gamma e^{-j\beta l}}$$

With $V_0^+$ known, every quantity in the problem is determined: the voltage and current at any point, the power delivered, the standing-wave pattern. The full recipe, in order:

  1. Find $\Gamma$ at the load from $Z_L$ and $Z_0$.
  2. Find $Z_{\text{in}}$ at the input using the tangent formula, or by rotating $\Gamma$ through $2\beta l$.
  3. Solve the lumped circuit for $\tilde{V}_{\text{in}}$ and $\tilde{I}_{\text{in}}$ with a voltage divider.
  4. Back out $V_0^+$, and from it anything else you need along the line.

Step 2 is the only one that involves transmission-line theory at all. Steps 1, 3 and 4 are algebra. That compression — an entire distributed structure reduced to one complex number — is what makes the subject workable.

Full derivation — power delivered, and the conjugate match

The average power delivered into the input plane, and on a lossless line therefore into the load, is

$$P = \tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}\left\{\tilde{V}_{\text{in}}\tilde{I}_{\text{in}}^{\,*}\right\} = \tfrac{1}{2}\left|\tilde{I}_{\text{in}}\right|^2 \operatorname{Re}\{Z_{\text{in}}\}$$

Substituting the divider result gives the power in terms of the generator alone:

$$P = \frac{1}{2}\,\frac{\left|\tilde{V}_g\right|^2 R_{\text{in}}}{\left|Z_g + Z_{\text{in}}\right|^2}$$

Maximising this over $Z_{\text{in}}$ gives the conjugate match condition $Z_{\text{in}} = Z_g^*$, which is the standard maximum-power-transfer result of circuit theory — here reached through a transmission line rather than a pair of wires.

It is worth being clear that this is a different condition from $Z_L = Z_0$. The first maximises power out of a given generator; the second eliminates the reflected wave. They coincide only when $Z_g = Z_0$, which is the usual arrangement in RF systems precisely because it makes the two goals agree. When they do not coincide, you must decide which one you actually want — and in a system with a long feed line, eliminating the reflection is almost always the right answer.

Two different matches

$Z_L = Z_0$ removes the reflected wave. $Z_{\text{in}} = Z_g^*$ takes the most power the source can give. They are the same condition only when $Z_g = Z_0$, which is why RF sources are built with outputs of the system impedance. When they differ, chapter 06 showed the other route: with both ends mismatched, a length of line alone can produce the conjugate match, but only when $|\Gamma_g| = |\Gamma_L|$.

A lossy line forgets its load

With loss, the input impedance uses the hyperbolic form from the derivation above, and $\Gamma$ at the input is $\Gamma_Le^{-2\alpha d}e^{-j2\beta d}$. It still turns clockwise, but it also shrinks, so the locus on the $\Gamma$ plane is a spiral into the centre, and $Z_{\text{in}}$ spirals onto $Z_0$:

$$Z_{\text{in}} = Z_0\,\frac{Z_L + Z_0\tanh\gamma d}{Z_0 + Z_L\tanh\gamma d} \;\longrightarrow\; Z_0 \quad\text{as}\quad \alpha d \to \infty$$ because $\tanh\gamma d \to 1$; whatever the load, a long enough lossy line looks matched

The return loss at the input is the load's return loss plus twice the line loss, which is chapter 07's lossy SWR seen from the impedance side. This is also the principle of a matched termination built from line alone: a long enough run of lossy cable, shorted or open at the far end, absorbs everything and reflects almost nothing.

Measuring Z0 and γ: short and open

The two special cases in the table give a classic way to measure an unknown line. Terminate a length $l$ of it in a short and measure $Z_{\text{sc}} = Z_0\tanh\gamma l$; then in an open and measure $Z_{\text{oc}} = Z_0\coth\gamma l$. Their product and ratio separate the two unknowns:

$$Z_0 = \sqrt{Z_{\text{sc}}\,Z_{\text{oc}}} \qquad\qquad \tanh\gamma l = \sqrt{\frac{Z_{\text{sc}}}{Z_{\text{oc}}}}$$ exact for any uniform line, lossy or not; the second gives $\alpha l$ directly, and $\beta l$ only modulo $\pi$

The ambiguity is genuine: $\tanh$ repeats every $j\pi$, so one pair of measurements cannot tell a line of electrical length $\beta l$ from one $\pi$ longer. Measuring at two nearby frequencies, or knowing the length roughly in advance, settles which branch is right. Network analysers do the same thing in effect, which is why their line-delay features ask for an approximate length.

Every lossless reactance rises with frequency

The short and the open sweep through every reactance as the length changes. Hold the length and change the frequency instead, and they still sweep through every value, but always in the same direction. That is a theorem, not a coincidence:

$$\frac{dX}{d\omega} > 0 \qquad\qquad \frac{dX}{d\omega} = \frac{4\left(W_m + W_e\right)}{|I|^2}$$ Foster's reactance theorem, for any lossless one-port: the slope is set by the total energy stored, so it can never be negative

Two consequences matter later. A reactance cannot pass through zero going downward, so poles and zeros of $X(\omega)$ must alternate: a shorted stub goes zero, pole, zero, pole as frequency rises. And a steeper slope means more stored energy, which means a narrower bandwidth: a matching network whose reactances change quickly with frequency is one storing a lot of energy, and it will hold its match only over a narrow band. Chapter 11's bandwidth comparisons are this theorem in practice.

A line section as a resonator

Near $f_0$ a shorted quarter-wave stub behaves like a parallel resonant circuit: its input impedance rises to a peak that is finite only because of loss. Expanding $\tanh\gamma l$ about the resonance gives the equivalent parallel RLC, and its quality factor in terms of the line's own constants:

$$Q = \frac{\beta}{2\alpha}$$ for a quarter-wave or half-wave TEM resonator; it depends only on the line, not on its length
Full derivation — the parallel RLC near resonance

Write $\beta l = \pi/2 + \pi\,\Delta\omega/2\omega_0$ near the resonance, and take $\alpha l \ll 1$. Using $\tanh(x + jy) = (\tanh x + j\tan y)/(1 + j\tanh x\tan y)$ with $\tan y \to -2\omega_0/\pi\Delta\omega$ near the pole, and keeping first-order terms,

$$Z_{\text{in}} = Z_0\tanh\gamma l \approx \frac{Z_0}{\alpha l + j\pi\Delta\omega/2\omega_0}$$

A parallel RLC near resonance has $Z \approx R/(1 + 2jQ\Delta\omega/\omega_0)$. Matching the two forms gives $R = Z_0/\alpha l$ and

$$Q = \frac{\pi}{4\alpha l} = \frac{\beta}{2\alpha}, \qquad \text{using}\quad \beta l = \frac{\pi}{2}$$

The same steps on a half-wave line, where $\beta l = \pi$, give $Q = \pi/2\alpha l$, which is again $\beta/2\alpha$. So the Q is a property of the line's loss per radian of phase, and a resonator can only be made sharper by making the line itself less lossy.

Air-filled coaxial resonators reach Q in the thousands; microstrip ones, with dielectric and radiation loss, a few hundred. This is chapter 03's ringing line seen in the frequency domain: with $R_s = 0$ and an open end the echoes never died, and the matching statement here is that a lossless line has infinite Q.

Self-check

Three questions on what the line does to a load.

1. A 50 Ω line is terminated in 50 Ω. Does its length matter?

A matched line has no reflected wave. $\Gamma$ is zero, and rotating zero leaves zero, so $Z_{\text{in}} = Z_0$ regardless of length. This is the only case where a cable behaves like an ideal wire, and it is why matching is worth the trouble — it removes length from the problem entirely.

2. Why is $Z_{\text{in}}$ periodic in $\lambda/2$ rather than $\lambda$?

The reflected wave covers each step out and back, so the relative phase advances at twice the rate of one wave. $\Gamma(d) = \Gamma_L e^{-j2\beta d}$ completes a full $2\pi$ when $d = \lambda/2$. The tangent's period of $\pi$ is not a coincidence but the same fact wearing different notation.

3. At a voltage minimum, $Z_{\text{in}} = Z_0/S$. Why is this purely real?

A minimum is precisely where the rotating $\Gamma$ crosses the negative real axis. Feeding a real $\Gamma$ through $Z_0(1+\Gamma)/(1-\Gamma)$ gives a real result. It holds for any load, reactive ones included — the minimum's position moves, but wherever it lands the impedance there is a pure resistance of $Z_0/S$.
