Chapter 03

The transmission line model

Kirchhoff's laws fail over a long wire but hold perfectly over a short one. So apply them to a piece short enough that they are exact, and let calculus assemble the pieces. Two coupled differential equations come out, and they govern every transmission line there is.

Assumes: chapter 02 — electrical length and why lumped theory fails.

Four parameters per metre

Any transmission line is two conductors running alongside each other: coaxial cable, a twisted pair, a track over a ground plane. Whatever the geometry, the same four effects are present along the whole length.

ParameterRepresentsUnitsPlaced
$R'$resistance of both conductorsΩ/min series
$L'$magnetic energy stored around the currentH/min series
$G'$leakage through the insulator between themS/min shunt
$C'$electric energy stored between the conductorsF/min shunt

Every one is quoted per unit length, which is what the primes mean, and that convention is doing real work. A piece of line twice as long has twice the series resistance and twice the shunt capacitance, so a slice of length $\Delta z$ contains $R'\Delta z$ ohms, $C'\Delta z$ farads, and so on. The model then scales to a line of any length without being rebuilt.

The two series elements govern what happens to the current flowing along the line; the two shunt elements govern what leaks across between the conductors. The two lossy ones, $R'$ and $G'$, remove energy; the two reactive ones, $L'$ and $C'$, only store it and hand it back.

Where the numbers come from

$R'$, $L'$, $G'$ and $C'$ are set entirely by the cross-sectional geometry and the materials — conductor spacing, diameter, the dielectric between them. Chapter 05 computes them for coax, twin-lead and microstrip. For this chapter they are simply four given numbers.

Kirchhoff on a slice

Take one slice running from $z$ to $z + \Delta z$. Because $\Delta z$ is small, the lumped approximation is valid inside it and the ordinary circuit laws apply.

Around the loop. The voltage entering is $v(z,t)$ and the voltage leaving is $v(z+\Delta z, t)$. The difference is whatever the series elements drop:

$$v(z,t) - v(z+\Delta z,t) = R'\Delta z\; i(z,t) + L'\Delta z\, \frac{\partial i(z,t)}{\partial t}$$

At the node. The current entering is $i(z,t)$ and the current leaving is $i(z+\Delta z,t)$. What went missing was diverted across the shunt elements:

$$i(z,t) - i(z+\Delta z,t) = G'\Delta z\; v(z+\Delta z,t) + C'\Delta z\, \frac{\partial v(z+\Delta z,t)}{\partial t}$$

Divide both by $\Delta z$ and let it shrink to zero. Each left-hand side becomes a derivative by definition, and the telegrapher's equations appear:

$$-\frac{\partial v(z,t)}{\partial z} = R'i(z,t) + L'\frac{\partial i(z,t)}{\partial t}$$ $$-\frac{\partial i(z,t)}{\partial z} = G'v(z,t) + C'\frac{\partial v(z,t)}{\partial t}$$ the telegrapher's equations, named for the problem they were first written to solve
Full derivation — the limit, carefully

Take the KVL statement and divide by $\Delta z$:

$$\frac{v(z,t) - v(z+\Delta z,t)}{\Delta z} = R'i(z,t) + L'\frac{\partial i(z,t)}{\partial t}$$

The left side is minus a difference quotient, so as $\Delta z \to 0$ it becomes $-\partial v/\partial z$ by the definition of the derivative. That gives the first equation exactly.

The KCL statement needs one extra remark. Its right-hand side was written with $v(z+\Delta z, t)$, since the shunt elements sit at the far end of the slice. Expand that in a Taylor series:

$$v(z+\Delta z, t) = v(z,t) + \Delta z\frac{\partial v}{\partial z} + O(\Delta z^2)$$

Substituting gives terms in $\Delta z$ and terms in $(\Delta z)^2$. After dividing by $\Delta z$ the second group still carries a factor of $\Delta z$ and vanishes in the limit, leaving

$$-\frac{\partial i}{\partial z} = G'v + C'\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}$$

This is why the placement of the shunt elements does not matter. Put them at the near end, the far end, or split them across both and the difference is second order in $\Delta z$ — gone in the limit. The same argument covers the choice of whether $R'$ and $L'$ sit in one conductor or are split between the two. The model has exactly one degree of freedom that survives: the four per-unit-length values.

Look at what these two equations say. Each one relates a change along the line to a rate of change in time. Voltage falls along $z$ because current is changing in time; current falls along $z$ because voltage is changing in time. Each quantity drives the other, exactly as $\vE$ and $\vH$ did in the prologue — and for the same underlying reason, since this is that physics in circuit notation.

In phasor form

Chapter 01 established that for a single-frequency steady state, $\partial/\partial t$ becomes multiplication by $j\omega$. Applying that removes time from the problem entirely and turns the partial derivatives in $z$ into ordinary ones:

$$-\frac{d\tilde{V}(z)}{dz} = (R' + j\omega L')\,\tilde{I}(z)$$ $$-\frac{d\tilde{I}(z)}{dz} = (G' + j\omega C')\,\tilde{V}(z)$$ two coupled first-order ordinary differential equations in one variable

The two bracketed groups appear so often that they earn names. $Z' = R' + j\omega L'$ is the series impedance per unit length; $Y' = G' + j\omega C'$ is the shunt admittance per unit length. In that shorthand the pair is simply

$$-\frac{d\tilde{V}}{dz} = Z'\tilde{I} \qquad\qquad -\frac{d\tilde{I}}{dz} = Y'\tilde{V}$$

Which is as compact as this subject gets. Two quantities, each one's slope set by the other. The next chapter decouples them the same way the prologue decoupled $\vE$ and $\vH$ — differentiate one and substitute the other — and out come the propagation constant and the characteristic impedance.

Increase the number of slices and the staircase smooths into a curve. That is the whole content of the limiting process: the ladder is a discrete approximation, the telegrapher's equations are what it becomes when the steps vanish. A real line has no slices — the parameters are genuinely continuous along it.

The lossless case

Most of this site works with $R' = G' = 0$. Over the short lengths used at microwave frequencies, good cable really does lose very little, and every important structural result — reflection, standing waves, impedance transformation, the Smith chart — survives the simplification unchanged. Loss changes the numbers, not the shape of the theory.

$$-\frac{\partial v}{\partial z} = L'\frac{\partial i}{\partial t} \qquad\qquad -\frac{\partial i}{\partial z} = C'\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}$$ the lossless telegrapher's equations — pure energy exchange between magnetic and electric storage

With the loss terms gone, the pair describes something purely conservative: energy shuttling between the magnetic field of the current and the electric field of the voltage, propagating without ever being dissipated. That is a wave, and chapter 04 confirms it by solving them.

Self-check

Three questions on what the model does and does not claim.

1. Why are $R'$, $L'$, $G'$ and $C'$ quoted per unit length rather than as plain circuit values?

The whole point is that these effects are distributed rather than located anywhere. Twice the length gives twice the capacitance and twice the series resistance, so only the per-metre quantity is a property of the line itself. It also makes the model independent of how finely you choose to slice.

2. The shunt elements were drawn at the far end of the slice. Would putting them at the near end change the result?

Expanding $v(z+\Delta z)$ in a Taylor series produces the same first-order terms either way; the placement only affects terms carrying an extra factor of $\Delta z$, which disappear when the slice shrinks. This is the sense in which the ladder is a scaffold: the final equations do not remember how it was built.

3. What do the telegrapher's equations say, physically?

Each equation couples a spatial derivative of one quantity to a time derivative of the other — the same mutual-regeneration structure as Faraday and Ampère in the prologue, which is unsurprising given where this model comes from. The fixed ratio $Z_0$ is a consequence derived in chapter 04, and it holds for a single travelling wave, not for the total voltage and current on a mismatched line.
