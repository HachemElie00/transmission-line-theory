Chapter 05

Microstrip and real lines

So far the four line parameters have been given numbers. They are not free: every one is fixed by the cross-section and the materials. This chapter computes them for the geometries you actually build, and looks at where microstrip stops obeying the model it was derived from.

Assumes: chapter 04 — $Z_0 = \sqrt{L'/C'}$ and $u_p = 1/\sqrt{L'C'}$.

Geometry sets everything

Chapter 04 closed with a result worth restating, because it organises this whole chapter. For any TEM line,

$$L'C' = \mu\varepsilon \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad u_p = \frac{1}{\sqrt{L'C'}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon}} = \frac{c}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}$$ the product is fixed by the material alone — the geometry cancels completely

So the cross-section cannot change the speed. Squeeze the conductors together and $C'$ rises while $L'$ falls in exact compensation. What the geometry does control is the ratio, and therefore $Z_0$:

$$Z_0 = \sqrt{\frac{L'}{C'}} \quad\text{(geometry)} \qquad\qquad u_p = \frac{c}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}} \quad\text{(material)}$$
Line$Z_0$ (lossless)Notes
Coaxial$\dfrac{\eta}{2\pi}\ln\dfrac{b}{a} = \dfrac{60}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}\ln\dfrac{b}{a}$$a$ inner radius, $b$ inner radius of the shield
Two-wire$\dfrac{\eta}{\pi}\cosh^{-1}\dfrac{D}{2a}$$a$ wire radius, $D$ centre spacing
Parallel plate$\dfrac{\eta\,d}{w}$$w \gg d$, fringing ignored
Microstripno closed formquasi-TEM — see below

Each of the first three is a genuine TEM structure: the fields sit entirely in one uniform dielectric, so $\varepsilon$ is unambiguous and the formulas are exact. A quick check on the coax expression — a 50 Ω cable with a PTFE dielectric ($\varepsilon_r = 2.1$) needs $\ln(b/a) = 50\sqrt{2.1}/60 = 1.21$, so $b/a \approx 3.35$. RG-142's datasheet gives 0.94 mm and 2.95 mm, a ratio of 3.1: its outer conductor is a braid, which acts as a slightly larger radius than the dielectric it is wound on.

Why microstrip is different

Microstrip is a track on top of a substrate with a ground plane underneath. It is the dominant line in practice — it is simply what a circuit board is — and it breaks the assumption every formula above relies on.

The field lines leaving the track do not all pass through the substrate. Some arc through the air above it. So the wave is travelling through two dielectrics at once, with different permittivities, and no single $\varepsilon$ describes it.

That has a real consequence: a pure TEM wave cannot exist on this structure. A true TEM mode needs the same phase velocity everywhere in the cross-section, and here the air region would propagate faster than the substrate region. The fields respond by acquiring small longitudinal components, which makes the mode quasi-TEM.

The engineering fix is a fiction that works extremely well: pretend the line is surrounded by one uniform imaginary dielectric, chosen so the line has the right capacitance. That number is the effective permittivity:

$$1 < \varepsilon_{\text{eff}} < \varepsilon_r$$ a weighted average of air and substrate — wide tracks push it toward $\varepsilon_r$, narrow ones toward air

Once you have $\varepsilon_{\text{eff}}$, every TEM result from chapter 04 applies with $\varepsilon_r$ replaced by it:

$$u_p = \frac{c}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\text{eff}}}} \qquad\qquad \lambda_g = \frac{\lambda_0}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\text{eff}}}} \qquad\qquad \beta = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda_g}$$
Why your quarter-wave stub is shorter than you expect

$\lambda_g$ is the guide wavelength, and it is what every physical length in this subject refers to. On FR-4 with $\varepsilon_{\text{eff}} \approx 3.3$, a quarter wave at 2.4 GHz is not $\lambda_0/4 = 31$ mm but $31/\sqrt{3.3} \approx 17$ mm. Using the free-space figure is among the most common and most expensive board errors.

The Hammerstad formulas

Microstrip has no exact closed form, but accurate curve fits exist. These are Hammerstad's, accurate to about 1 % over the usual range, and they are what most tools use internally. Everything depends on the single ratio $W/h$ — track width over substrate thickness.

$$\varepsilon_{\text{eff}} = \frac{\varepsilon_r + 1}{2} + \frac{\varepsilon_r - 1}{2}\left(1 + \frac{12h}{W}\right)^{-1/2}$$ for $W/h < 1$, add $\;0.04\left(1 - W/h\right)^2$ times $(\varepsilon_r-1)/2$

Then the impedance, in two branches:

$$\frac{W}{h} \le 1: \qquad Z_0 = \frac{60}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\text{eff}}}}\,\ln\!\left(\frac{8h}{W} + \frac{W}{4h}\right)$$ $$\frac{W}{h} \ge 1: \qquad Z_0 = \frac{120\pi}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\text{eff}}}\left[\dfrac{W}{h} + 1.393 + 0.667\ln\!\left(\dfrac{W}{h} + 1.444\right)\right]}$$

The structure of both is worth reading even if you never evaluate them by hand. Wider track means lower impedance — more capacitance to the ground plane, less inductance. And $\varepsilon_{\text{eff}}$ sits under a square root in the denominator of each, so a higher-permittivity substrate lowers $Z_0$ for the same geometry.

Try the narrow and wide presets and watch two things move together: as the track narrows, $Z_0$ climbs and $\varepsilon_{\text{eff}}$ falls. The second happens because a narrow track holds proportionally more of its field in the air, dragging the effective permittivity down toward 1.

Move the frequency slider and the curve does not budge — only $\lambda_g$ and $\lambda_g/4$ respond. That is not an oversight in the figure; it is what the formulas say. Hammerstad's $\varepsilon_{\text{eff}}$ and $Z_0$ contain no frequency term, so in this model a track is 50 Ω at DC and at 10 GHz alike. Frequency enters only through $\lambda_g = \lambda_0/\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\text{eff}}}$, which is what sets every physical length you cut. Real microstrip does drift with frequency — that is dispersion, and it is the first entry in the table below.

Full derivation — synthesis, going the other way

Design usually runs backwards: you know the $Z_0$ you want and need $W/h$. Hammerstad's synthesis formulas invert the analysis ones. First compute two intermediate quantities from the target impedance:

$$A = \frac{Z_0}{60}\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_r+1}{2}} + \frac{\varepsilon_r-1}{\varepsilon_r+1}\left(0.23 + \frac{0.11}{\varepsilon_r}\right)$$
$$B = \frac{377\pi}{2 Z_0 \sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}$$

Then for a narrow track, $W/h < 2$:

$$\frac{W}{h} = \frac{8e^{A}}{e^{2A} - 2}$$

and for a wide one, $W/h > 2$:

$$\frac{W}{h} = \frac{2}{\pi}\left[B - 1 - \ln(2B-1) + \frac{\varepsilon_r-1}{2\varepsilon_r}\left(\ln(B-1) + 0.39 - \frac{0.61}{\varepsilon_r}\right)\right]$$

Compute both, keep whichever lands in its own validity range, then feed the answer back through the analysis formulas to check. The two branches disagree slightly near $W/h = 2$, and the round trip is the only honest way to know which one you should have used.

A worked case. For 50 Ω on FR-4 ($\varepsilon_r = 4.4$): $A = 1.53$, giving $W/h = 1.91$; and $B = 5.65$, giving $W/h = 1.91$. The two branches agree to better than a tenth of a percent here, and the round trip confirms it — feeding $W/h = 1.91$ back through the analysis formula returns 50.3 Ω. So with $h = 1.6$ mm the track is about 3.0 mm wide. That is the number every FR-4 board you have seen is built around.

Checking the fit against the field

Hammerstad's formulas are curve fits, so it is worth seeing them confirmed by something that is not a fit. Part two showed that the transverse field of a TEM line solves Laplace's equation in the cross-section, and quasi-TEM microstrip is close enough that the same calculation gives its parameters. Solve it twice, once with the substrate and once with everything replaced by air:

$$\varepsilon_{\text{eff}} = \frac{C'}{C'_{\text{air}}} \qquad\qquad Z_0 = \frac{1}{c\sqrt{C'\,C'_{\text{air}}}}$$ $C'$ from the charge on the track per volt, found by Gauss's law around it; the air solve fixes $L'$, since a dielectric does not change the inductance
Full derivation — why two capacitances are enough

A non-magnetic dielectric changes the electric field but not the magnetic one, so $L'$ is the same with or without the substrate. With the substrate replaced by air the line is a true TEM line in vacuum, for which $L'C'_{\text{air}} = \mu_0\varepsilon_0 = 1/c^2$, so $L' = 1/(c^2C'_{\text{air}})$. With the real substrate, the speed is $1/\sqrt{L'C'}$ and the effective permittivity is defined by $u_p = c/\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\text{eff}}}$:

$$\varepsilon_{\text{eff}} = c^2L'C' = \frac{C'}{C'_{\text{air}}}, \qquad Z_0 = \sqrt{\frac{L'}{C'}} = \frac{1}{c\sqrt{C'C'_{\text{air}}}}$$

At the boundary between the substrate and the air the normal component of $\vD$ is continuous (part one), which the solver enforces by using the average permittivity on the grid row that lies on the interface.

The two routes agree to within 3 % across the range, which is the size of Hammerstad's own stated accuracy plus the grid and the box; the largest gap is at the narrowest track, only three cells wide. They share nothing but the geometry. A field solver of exactly this kind, refined, is what a commercial tool runs when the closed-form fits are no longer good enough.

Where the model stops

Everything so far treats microstrip as an ideal quasi-TEM line with frequency-independent parameters. Four effects break that, and knowing when each one bites is the difference between a board that works and one that needs a second spin.

EffectWhat happensWhen it matters
Dispersion$\varepsilon_{\text{eff}}$ rises with frequency as more field is pulled into the substrate, so $Z_0$ and $u_p$ driftabove roughly a few GHz on thick substrates
Skin effectcurrent crowds into a surface layer of depth $\delta = 1/\sqrt{\pi f\mu\sigma}$, so $R'$ grows as $\sqrt{f}$always at RF; dominates conductor loss
Radiationan open structure leaks, especially at discontinuities, bends and open stubsworse on thick, low-$\varepsilon_r$ substrates
Higher-order modesabove a cutoff the quasi-TEM mode is no longer alonewhen $h$ approaches $\lambda_g/2$

Skin effect deserves a line of its own because it explains a fact every RF engineer meets. At 1 GHz in copper the skin depth is about 2 µm — the current is confined to a film far thinner than the conductor. Making the track thicker buys almost nothing; making it wider, or smoother, is what reduces loss. It is also why $R'$ in chapter 04 is not really a constant: it climbs as $\sqrt{f}$.

The practical summary

Below a few GHz on ordinary board, the formulas above are good to a percent or two and you can design directly from them. Above that, use them to get close and then let a field solver finish the job — but the intuition they encode, that $W/h$ sets impedance and $\varepsilon_{\text{eff}}$ sets wavelength, never stops being true.

Dispersion, quantified

The first entry in that table can be put in numbers. As frequency rises, the field is drawn further into the substrate, the high-permittivity region, and $\varepsilon_{\text{eff}}$ climbs from its static value toward $\varepsilon_r$. Getsinger's model captures this with one characteristic frequency set by the impedance and the substrate thickness:

$$\varepsilon_{\text{eff}}(f) = \varepsilon_r - \frac{\varepsilon_r - \varepsilon_{\text{eff}}(0)}{1 + G\,(f/f_p)^2}, \qquad f_p = \frac{Z_0}{2\mu_0 h}, \quad G = 0.6 + 0.009\,Z_0$$ $Z_0$ in ohms; an empirical fit, good to a few percent, which is enough to show where dispersion starts

Thickness is what matters: $f_p$ scales as $1/h$, so a substrate a sixth as thick disperses at six times the frequency. That is one reason millimetre-wave boards use thin substrates, and why the "few GHz" in the table above is a statement about 1.6 mm FR-4, not about microstrip in general.

The open end is longer than it looks

The field does not stop where the copper stops. It fringes past the end of an open track and stores extra charge there, which makes the end behave as if the line continued for a short distance $\Delta l$. A stub cut to exactly $\lambda_g/4$ is therefore electrically too long, and resonates low.

$$\frac{\Delta l}{h} = 0.412\,\frac{(\varepsilon_{\text{eff}} + 0.3)(W/h + 0.264)}{(\varepsilon_{\text{eff}} - 0.258)(W/h + 0.8)}$$ Hammerstad and Bekkadal's fit; cut the stub short by $\Delta l$ to land on frequency

Bends and width steps disturb the field the same way and need the same kind of correction. A sharp right-angle bend adds excess capacitance at its outer corner, which is why bends are mitred, removing somewhat more than half of the corner along its diagonal. A step in width adds a small shunt capacitance at the junction. None of these appear in the transmission-line model itself; they are lumped corrections stitched onto it, and every microstrip design tool carries a library of them.

Stripline, coplanar waveguide and coupled lines

Microstrip is one of several planar lines, and the differences between them come straight back to whether the field sits in one material or two.

Stripline buries the track in one uniform dielectric between two grounds. The field never sees air, so it is a true TEM line: $\varepsilon_{\text{eff}} = \varepsilon_r$ exactly, there is no dispersion of the microstrip kind, and the closed form is good. For a track of width $W$ between grounds $b$ apart,

$$Z_0 = \frac{30\pi}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}\,\frac{b}{W_e + 0.441\,b}, \qquad W_e = W - \left(0.35 - \frac{W}{b}\right)^2 b \;\text{ for } W/b < 0.35, \text{ else } W_e = W$$

Coplanar waveguide puts the grounds on the same surface as the track, separated by two narrow slots. It needs no vias to reach ground, which makes it natural for mounting components and for probing a chip, and on a thick substrate half its field is in air, giving $\varepsilon_{\text{eff}} \approx (\varepsilon_r + 1)/2$.

Coupled lines are two tracks close enough to share field. They support two modes: the even mode, both at the same potential, and the odd mode, at opposite potentials. Any pair of signals is a sum of the two, and each mode has its own impedance, $Z_{0e}$ and $Z_{0o}$, with $Z_{0e} > Z_{0o}$ because in the odd mode the tracks' fields terminate on each other. The coupling coefficient is

$$k = \frac{Z_{0e} - Z_{0o}}{Z_{0e} + Z_{0o}}$$

which is what a directional coupler is built from and what crosstalk between neighbouring tracks is made of. In microstrip the odd mode has more of its field in air than the even mode, so the two travel at different speeds; the difference converts a signal on one track into a pulse at the far end of the other. In stripline both modes travel at the same speed and that far-end crosstalk cancels, one of the main reasons sensitive high-speed signals are routed on inner layers.

Self-check

Three questions on what geometry controls.

1. You move a coax design to a substrate with twice the permittivity, keeping the same $b/a$. What happens to $Z_0$ and to $u_p$?

$Z_0 = (60/\sqrt{\varepsilon_r})\ln(b/a)$ and $u_p = c/\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}$, so both scale as $1/\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}$ and both fall to about 71 % of what they were — a 29 % drop, not 41 %, since dividing by $\sqrt{2}$ is not the same as subtracting $\sqrt{2}-1$. The geometry term $\ln(b/a)$ is untouched — which is exactly the split this chapter opened with: geometry sets the ratio, material sets the speed.

2. Why does microstrip need an effective permittivity at all?

It is a two-dielectric problem. Part of the field arcs through air above the track, part passes through the substrate, and the mode is a compromise between them — which is also why it is quasi-TEM rather than TEM. $\varepsilon_{\text{eff}}$ is the single uniform permittivity that would give the same line capacitance, and it always lands between 1 and $\varepsilon_r$.

3. A stub on FR-4 needs to be a quarter wavelength at 2.4 GHz. Roughly how long?

Free space gives $\lambda_0 = 125$ mm, so $\lambda_0/4 = 31$ mm. But the wave travels in the guide, not in air: $\lambda_g = \lambda_0/\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\text{eff}}}$, and with $\varepsilon_{\text{eff}} \approx 3.3$ that is about 69 mm, giving a quarter wave near 17 mm. Note it is $\varepsilon_{\text{eff}}$ under the root, not $\varepsilon_r$ — using 4.4 would give 15 mm and a stub tuned to the wrong frequency.
