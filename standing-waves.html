Chapter 07

Standing waves

A standing wave is not a kind of wave. It is the two travelling waves of the last chapter, caught in the act of adding up. Everything about minima, maxima and SWR follows from that, and from one observation: their relative phase depends on where you stand, never on when you look.

Assumes: chapter 06 — $\Gamma$, and $\Gamma(l) = \Gamma_L e^{-j2\beta l}$.

Two waves, one line

Measure distance $l$ back from the load. The total phasor voltage is the sum of the two waves, and one factoring step does all the work:

$$\tilde{V}(l) = V_0^+\left(e^{j\beta l} + \Gamma e^{-j\beta l}\right) = V_0^+e^{j\beta l}\left(1 + \Gamma e^{-j2\beta l}\right)$$

The first factor has magnitude one, so it contributes nothing measurable. What is left is the whole subject:

$$|V(l)| = |V_0^+|\;\bigl|1 + \Gamma e^{-j2\beta l}\bigr|$$ one, plus a rotating vector of length $|\Gamma|$ — that is the entire standing wave

Picture it in the complex plane: a fixed unit vector, and a second vector of length $|\Gamma|$ whose angle depends on where you stand. When they point the same way the lengths add; when they oppose, they subtract.

$$|V|_{\max} = |V_0^+|\left(1 + |\Gamma|\right) \qquad\qquad |V|_{\min} = |V_0^+|\left(1 - |\Gamma|\right)$$

Crucially, this depends only on $l$, never on $t$. The pattern is fixed in space. Each point on the line simply oscillates at its own fixed amplitude.

Standing, travelling, and in between

Every point on the line oscillates. What distinguishes the three cases is whether the pattern translates.

Pure travelling

|Γ| = 0

Nothing returns. The envelope is flat and every point sees the same amplitude. All power is absorbed.

Partially standing

|Γ| = 0.5

The envelope is fixed and ripples, but the wave still drifts and the minima never reach zero. Part travelling, part standing.

Pure standing

|Γ| = 1

Everything reflects. True nodes sit still at zero for all time while the bellies breathe in place. No net power reaches the load.

The triangles mark the instantaneous zero crossings. In the first two panels they march; in the third they are pinned. Split the total into a travelling part of amplitude $1-|\Gamma|$ and a standing part of amplitude $2|\Gamma|$. The standing part is two equal waves running opposite ways, so it carries no net power at all; whatever reaches the load is carried by the travelling remainder. Only at $|\Gamma| = 1$ does that remainder vanish, and anything in between is a partially standing wave.

One trap in that split, because it is tempting and wrong: the delivered power is not $(1-|\Gamma|)^2$. Power is quadratic, so the two parts interfere, and the cross term between them carries real power of its own — at $|\Gamma| = 0.5$ the travelling part accounts for only a third of what actually arrives. Add all three contributions and you recover chapter 06's $1-|\Gamma|^2$, which remains the delivered fraction. The decomposition tells you which part of the pattern moves, not how the power divides.

Why the pattern holds still

Stand at one fixed position and watch. Both waves oscillate at the same $\omega$, so they are two phasors spinning together at the same rate. Their individual phases change constantly — that is what oscillating means — but their difference does not:

$$\text{incident phase} - \text{reflected phase} = (\omega t + \beta l) - (\omega t - \beta l + \theta_r) = 2\beta l - \theta_r$$ the $\omega t$ cancels — both hands of the clock spin, the angle between them does not

So every position on the line carries a permanent verdict about how the two waves get along there, and that verdict is baked into the geometry. It cannot move. That is the whole mystery of why a standing wave stands.

A common objection — but the gap between the curves does change

It does, and it is worth being precise about why that is not a contradiction. Phase difference is a horizontal relationship — how long after one wave peaks the other peaks. The vertical gap between two curves is a difference of two heights, and both heights are oscillating, so the gap oscillates too.

Test it at a voltage maximum, say $l = 0.375\lambda$ with $\theta_r = -90°$. There $\beta l = 135°$, so the reflected wave is $0.5\cos(\omega t - 225°)$ — and $-225°$ is the same angle as $+135°$. The two waves are exactly in phase, which is why that point is a maximum:

$$v^+ = \cos(\omega t + 135°) \qquad v^- = 0.5\cos(\omega t + 135°) = \tfrac{1}{2}v^+$$

Yet the gap $v^+ - v^-= \tfrac12 v^+$ swings from $+0.5$ through $0$ to $-0.5$ across a cycle. Two perfectly in-phase waves, and the vertical gap still moves.

Look at the ratio instead: it is $0.5$, always. And horizontally, both curves cross zero at the same instants and peak at the same instants, forever. In general the difference of two same-frequency sinusoids is itself a sinusoid of amplitude $\sqrt{1+|\Gamma|^2 - 2|\Gamma|\cos\Delta\phi}$ — so what is constant is the amplitude of the gap, never the gap itself.

The analogy that settles it: two runners on a hilly track, same pace, one ten metres ahead. Their separation along the track never changes — that is the phase. Their difference in altitude changes constantly as they crest hills and drop into dips.

The factor of two in $2\beta l$ also hands you the quarter-wave rule for free. It is the round trip: the reflected wave covers each step out and back, so the relative phase slips at twice the rate of a single wave. Step a quarter wavelength and it flips a full half turn:

$$2\beta\cdot\frac{\lambda}{4} = 2\left(\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\right)\frac{\lambda}{4} = \pi = 180°$$

Everything between interpolates smoothly, by the law of cosines:

$$|V| = |V_0^+|\sqrt{1 + |\Gamma|^2 + 2|\Gamma|\cos\left(\theta_r - 2\beta l\right)}$$ that single square root is the envelope — the conditions above are just where the cosine hits $\pm1$
Full derivation — where the square root comes from

Start from $|V(l)| = |V_0^+|\,|1 + \Gamma e^{-j2\beta l}|$, derived above, and write $\Gamma = |\Gamma|e^{j\theta_r}$ so the rotating term collects into one angle:

$$\Gamma e^{-j2\beta l} = |\Gamma|\,e^{j(\theta_r - 2\beta l)} = |\Gamma|\cos\phi + j|\Gamma|\sin\phi, \qquad \phi \equiv \theta_r - 2\beta l$$

So the bracket is a single complex number with a known real and imaginary part:

$$1 + \Gamma e^{-j2\beta l} = \bigl(1 + |\Gamma|\cos\phi\bigr) + j\,|\Gamma|\sin\phi$$

Its magnitude is Pythagoras on those two parts:

$$\bigl|1 + \Gamma e^{-j2\beta l}\bigr|^2 = \bigl(1 + |\Gamma|\cos\phi\bigr)^2 + \bigl(|\Gamma|\sin\phi\bigr)^2 = 1 + 2|\Gamma|\cos\phi + |\Gamma|^2\cos^2\phi + |\Gamma|^2\sin^2\phi$$

The last two terms collapse by $\cos^2\phi + \sin^2\phi = 1$, leaving

$$\bigl|1 + \Gamma e^{-j2\beta l}\bigr|^2 = 1 + |\Gamma|^2 + 2|\Gamma|\cos\phi$$

and the square root of that, times $|V_0^+|$, is the boxed result. This is the law of cosines in disguise: two vectors of length $1$ and $|\Gamma|$ meeting at angle $\phi$ sum to a vector whose squared length is exactly $1^2 + |\Gamma|^2 + 2(1)|\Gamma|\cos\phi$ — the same picture from the start of this section, now with the algebra behind it. Setting $\phi = 0$ or $\phi = \pi$ recovers $1 \pm |\Gamma|$ without any further work, which is why those two cases needed no derivation above.

Standing wave ratio

Nobody can measure $|V_0^+|$ directly. A ratio cancels it, which is why SWR is the number every lab reports:

$$S = \frac{|V|_{\max}}{|V|_{\min}} = \frac{1 + |\Gamma|}{1 - |\Gamma|} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad |\Gamma| = \frac{S-1}{S+1}$$ real, positive, needs no phase reference and no absolute calibration

Note that $S$ carries only $|\Gamma|$ — the phase is thrown away. Two quite different loads can have identical SWR, which is exactly why a single SWR reading cannot identify a load, and why the position of a minimum has to be measured too.

Reading a line backwards

A detector cannot follow the carrier; it reads the envelope. That is enough, because the envelope's shape encodes $\Gamma$ completely. This figure is the one to keep in front of you.

Maxima occur where the rotating vector has zero net phase, minima where it is exactly opposed:

$$\theta_r - 2\beta l_{\max} = 2n\pi \qquad\qquad \theta_r - 2\beta l_{\min} = \pm(2n+1)\pi$$

Which gives four steps that never change:

  1. Spacing gives $\lambda$. Successive minima are $\lambda/2$ apart; a minimum and its neighbouring maximum are $\lambda/4$ apart. Read the question carefully — this is where most marks are lost. Then $\beta = 2\pi/\lambda$.
  2. Ratio gives $|\Gamma|$. $|\Gamma| = (S-1)/(S+1)$.
  3. Position gives the phase. Anchor on a minimum: $\theta_r = 2\beta l_{\min} - \pi$. Minima are sharp and deep; maxima are broad and flat, so their position is far less certain. Every slotted-line procedure is written around $l_{\min}$.
  4. Un-map to the load. $Z_L = Z_0\dfrac{1+\Gamma}{1-\Gamma}$.

A closed form for step 4 that avoids complex division under exam pressure:

$$Z_L = Z_0\,\frac{\left(1 - |\Gamma|^2\right) + j\,2|\Gamma|\sin\theta_r}{1 - 2|\Gamma|\cos\theta_r + |\Gamma|^2}$$

And a check worth teaching: at a voltage minimum the impedance looking toward the load is purely real and equals $Z_0/S$; at a maximum it is $S\,Z_0$. Any answer can be verified in seconds with that — and chapter 08 derives why.

One picture holds the whole method

Moving along the line rotates $\Gamma$ clockwise about the origin, one lap per $\lambda/2$. A maximum is where you cross the positive real axis, a minimum where you cross the negative one — half a lap apart, hence $\lambda/4$. That is the Smith chart in embryo, and once the rotation is automatic these problems stop being algebra.

Self-check

Three questions on the pattern.

1. Two loads give the same SWR of 3. Are they the same load?

$S$ depends on $|\Gamma|$ alone. Every load on the circle $|\Gamma| = 0.5$ — an infinite family — gives $S = 3$. To pin down which one, you need the phase, and the standard way to get it is the position of a voltage minimum. This is why slotted-line measurements always record both.

2. At $|\Gamma| = 0.5$, do the minima reach zero?

The minimum is $|V_0^+|(1-|\Gamma|)$, which is zero only when $|\Gamma| = 1$. At $|\Gamma| = 0.5$ the envelope dips to half the incident amplitude and no lower. True nulls require total reflection — a short, an open, or a pure reactance.

3. A slotted line shows successive minima 14 cm and 24 cm from the load. What is $\lambda$?

Like extrema repeat every $\lambda/2$, so the 10 cm spacing gives $\lambda = 20$ cm. The $\lambda/4$ rule applies between a minimum and its neighbouring maximum, which are unlike. Mixing these up is the single most common error in slotted-line problems — always check which pair you were handed.
