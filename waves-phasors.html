Chapter 01

Waves, phasors and complex numbers

Everything that follows is written in one notation, and it is worth earning that notation properly rather than meeting it as a convention. A wave depends on two variables; a phasor removes one of them. What that costs, and what it buys, decides the shape of the whole subject.

Assumes: sine and cosine, and that $j^2 = -1$. Nothing else.

What makes something a wave

A quantity that varies in time alone is an oscillation. A quantity that varies in space alone is a pattern. A wave is what you get when the two are locked together, so that the pattern in space shifts as time passes:

$$v(z,t) = A\cos(\omega t - \beta z + \phi)$$ $A$ amplitude · $\omega$ angular frequency, rad/s · $\beta$ phase constant, rad/m · $\phi$ a fixed head start

The two terms inside the cosine carry different units and different meanings, and keeping them straight is most of the battle. $\omega t$ is how far through the cycle you are. $\beta z$ is how far along you are standing. Their combination is what travels.

To see why, track a point of constant phase — a particular crest. Constant phase means $\omega t - \beta z$ does not change, so as $t$ advances, $z$ must advance with it:

$$\omega t - \beta z = \text{constant} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad z = \frac{\omega}{\beta}t + \text{const} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad u_p = \frac{\omega}{\beta}$$

That is the phase velocity: the speed at which a crest moves. And the minus sign is the whole story of direction. A $-\beta z$ makes the crest move toward $+z$; flip it to $+\beta z$ and the crest moves backwards. Those two signs are the forward and reverse waves that every later chapter is built on.

Two periodicities, one wave. In space the pattern repeats every wavelength; in time it repeats every period. They are tied together by the speed:

QuantityDefinitionRelation
Wavelength $\lambda$distance per cycle$\lambda = 2\pi/\beta$
Period $T$time per cycle$T = 2\pi/\omega$
Frequency $f$cycles per second$f = 1/T = \omega/2\pi$
Phase velocity $u_p$speed of a crest$u_p = \omega/\beta = f\lambda$
The relation that decides everything later

$\lambda = u_p / f$. At 50 Hz a wavelength is thousands of kilometres, so no laboratory wire is an appreciable fraction of one. At 5 GHz in a typical dielectric it is a few centimetres, and a connector lead is. Chapter 02 is entirely about that comparison.

Complex numbers, quickly

Only one property of complex numbers actually matters here, and it is Euler's identity:

$$e^{j\theta} = \cos\theta + j\sin\theta$$ so $\cos\theta = \operatorname{Re}\{e^{j\theta}\}$, which is the bridge to everything below

Read geometrically, $e^{j\theta}$ is a unit vector at angle $\theta$ in the complex plane. Multiplying by $Ae^{j\phi}$ stretches it by $A$ and rotates it by $\phi$. That single picture — a number as an arrow with a length and an angle — is why complex notation suits waves so well: a wave also has a size and a phase, and nothing else.

Full derivation — the identities worth having fluent

Multiplication in polar form multiplies magnitudes and adds angles:

$$z_1 z_2 = |z_1||z_2|\,e^{j(\theta_1+\theta_2)} \qquad \frac{z_1}{z_2} = \frac{|z_1|}{|z_2|}e^{j(\theta_1-\theta_2)}$$

This is why reflection coefficients are so much easier in polar form: moving along a line multiplies $\Gamma$ by a unit-magnitude factor, which leaves $|\Gamma|$ untouched and only turns the angle.

Special values worth recognising instantly, since they appear constantly as phase shifts:

$$e^{j\pi/2} = j \qquad e^{j\pi} = -1 \qquad e^{-j\pi/2} = -j \qquad e^{j2\pi} = 1$$

So multiplying by $j$ is a quarter turn anticlockwise, and multiplying by $-1$ is a half turn — which is exactly the 180° flip a short circuit applies to a reflected wave.

The complex conjugate reflects across the real axis, $z^* = x - jy = |z|e^{-j\theta}$, and gives the magnitude through

$$z z^* = |z|^2$$

which is the trick behind rationalising every $\dfrac{1+\Gamma}{1-\Gamma}$ you will meet from chapter 06 onwards: multiply top and bottom by the conjugate of the denominator and the denominator becomes real.

The phasor

Here is the problem phasors solve. Write out a circuit equation for a sinusoid and you are differentiating cosines, which turn into sines, which turn into negative cosines. The algebra grows, and none of it is the physics — it is bookkeeping on a time dependence that is the same everywhere in the circuit.

So factor it out. Every quantity in a time-harmonic problem — one driven at a single frequency — can be written as

$$v(z,t) = \operatorname{Re}\left\{ \tilde{V}(z)\,e^{j\omega t} \right\}$$ $\tilde{V}$ is the phasor: a complex number holding amplitude and phase, with the time dependence removed

For the travelling wave at the top of this page, that means:

$$v(z,t) = A\cos(\omega t - \beta z + \phi) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \tilde{V}(z) = A e^{j\phi} e^{-j\beta z}$$

The payoff is immediate. Because $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}e^{j\omega t} = j\omega e^{j\omega t}$, differentiation in time stops being calculus and becomes multiplication:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial t} \;\longrightarrow\; j\omega \qquad\qquad \int dt \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{1}{j\omega}$$ every differential equation in time becomes an algebraic equation in $j\omega$

That substitution is what makes impedance possible. An inductor's $v = L\,di/dt$ becomes $\tilde{V} = j\omega L\,\tilde{I}$, so it has an impedance $j\omega L$ — a single complex number relating voltage to current, exactly as a resistor has $R$. Without phasors there is no such thing as impedance, and without impedance there is no characteristic impedance, no $\Gamma$, and no Smith chart.

What it costs

Phasors describe the steady state at one frequency and nothing else. Switch-on, pulses, steps and anything transient are outside their reach — those need the time-domain equations or a superposition of many frequencies. For a driven sinusoid, though, the phasor answer is exact, not approximate.

Full derivation — why taking Re at the end is legitimate

The move looks suspicious: we drop $\operatorname{Re}\{\cdot\}$, do all the algebra on complex quantities, then reinstate it at the end. That is valid, but only because of a specific property.

Taking the real part is a linear operation. For any complex $z_1, z_2$ and any real constants $a, b$:

$$\operatorname{Re}\{a z_1 + b z_2\} = a\operatorname{Re}\{z_1\} + b\operatorname{Re}\{z_2\}$$

So it commutes with addition, with scaling by real numbers, and with differentiation and integration in time. Any equation built only from those operations can be solved in the complex domain and have its real part taken at the very end.

Where it breaks. It does not commute with multiplication of two time-varying quantities, because

$$\operatorname{Re}\{z_1\}\cdot\operatorname{Re}\{z_2\} \neq \operatorname{Re}\{z_1 z_2\}$$

which matters the moment you compute power, since power is $v \times i$. That is exactly why average power carries its own formula with a conjugate and a factor of one half,

$$P_{av} = \tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}\{\tilde{V}\tilde{I}^{\,*}\}$$

rather than simply being the product of the two phasors. It is also why nonlinear devices — mixers, diodes, amplifiers driven into compression — cannot be analysed with phasors at all: they generate frequencies that were not in the input, and a single-frequency description has nowhere to put them.

Where the numbers land

The same equations describe the entire electromagnetic spectrum; only the numbers change. What changes with them is which approximations survive.

Two cautions on that table. Wavelength shortens inside a dielectric by $\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}$, so a 12 cm free-space wave is nearer 6 cm on ordinary circuit board — which is why the numbers in chapter 05 come out smaller than intuition suggests. And nothing physical changes at any row of that table. There is no frequency at which circuit theory switches off. What changes is only the ratio of your hardware to $\lambda$, which is a continuous quantity, and the next chapter is about where in that continuum the error becomes intolerable.

Self-check

Three questions on the notation rather than the arithmetic.

1. Two waves are written $\cos(\omega t - \beta z)$ and $\cos(\omega t + \beta z)$. What differs?

Follow a point of constant phase. For the first, $\omega t - \beta z$ constant needs $z$ to increase as $t$ does. For the second, $\omega t + \beta z$ constant needs $z$ to decrease. Same $\beta$, so the same wavelength; only the direction of travel differs. Those two signs become the incident and reflected waves in chapter 06.

2. Why can't phasors be used to analyse a mixer or a diode?

A phasor is shorthand for "amplitude and phase at frequency $\omega$". Nonlinear devices produce output at frequencies absent from the input — harmonics, sum and difference products — and a single-frequency description has nowhere to represent them. Complex impedance is not the problem; phasors handle that routinely.

3. Average power is $\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}\{\tilde{V}\tilde{I}^{\,*}\}$ rather than simply $\tilde{V}\tilde{I}$. Why the extra machinery?

The whole phasor method rests on $\operatorname{Re}\{\cdot\}$ being linear, which lets you postpone it to the end. Multiplication breaks that: $\operatorname{Re}\{z_1\}\operatorname{Re}\{z_2\} \neq \operatorname{Re}\{z_1z_2\}$. Power is a product of two time-varying quantities, so it needs its own correctly derived formula. The factor of one half does happen to encode the peak-to-RMS conversion, but that is a consequence, not the reason.
