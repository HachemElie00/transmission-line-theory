The wave equation and Helmholtz

Chapter 01 argued in words that the two curl laws feed each other. Here that argument is made algebraically: take the curl of one, substitute the other, and the coupled pair collapses into a single equation whose solutions travel at a speed fixed entirely by the medium.

Decoupling the two curl equations

The obstacle is that $\vE$ and $\vH$ appear in both equations at once. Faraday gives $\vE$ in terms of $\vH$; Ampère gives $\vH$ in terms of $\vE$. Neither can be solved alone.

The trick is to take the curl a second time. Doing so on Faraday's law produces a term in $\curl\vH$, which Ampère's law lets you replace — and $\vH$ vanishes from the problem entirely.

In a source-free region ($\rho_v = 0$, $\vJ = 0$) the result is:

$$\nabla^2 \vE = \mu\varepsilon\,\frac{\partial^2 \vE}{\partial t^2}$$ the vector wave equation — and an identical one holds for $\vH$
Full derivation — curl twice and substitute

Start from Faraday's law and take the curl of both sides:

$$\curl(\curl \vE) = \curl\left(-\frac{\partial \vB}{\partial t}\right) = -\mu\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\curl \vH)$$

The time derivative and the curl commute because they act on different variables, which is what lets them be swapped. Now substitute Ampère–Maxwell with no conduction current, $\curl \vH = \varepsilon\,\partial\vE/\partial t$:

$$\curl(\curl \vE) = -\mu\varepsilon\,\frac{\partial^2 \vE}{\partial t^2}$$

$\vH$ is gone. Now expand the left side with the standard vector identity

$$\curl(\curl \vA) = \nabla(\dive \vA) - \nabla^2 \vA$$

In a source-free region Gauss's law gives $\dive \vD = 0$, and with $\varepsilon$ uniform that means $\dive \vE = 0$, so the first term vanishes:

$$-\nabla^2 \vE = -\mu\varepsilon\,\frac{\partial^2 \vE}{\partial t^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \nabla^2 \vE = \mu\varepsilon\,\frac{\partial^2 \vE}{\partial t^2}$$

The same steps starting from Ampère's law instead give the identical equation for $\vH$. Note where the assumptions entered: source-free killed the gradient term, and homogeneous let $\varepsilon$ and $\mu$ pass through the derivatives. In a medium whose properties vary with position, the $\nabla(\dive\vE)$ term survives and the two field components stay coupled.

Compare it against the standard form of a wave equation, $\nabla^2 f = \dfrac{1}{u^2}\dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial t^2}$, and the propagation speed reads off immediately:

$$u = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon}}$$ in free space, $\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}$ H/m and $\varepsilon_0 \approx 8.854\times10^{-12}$ F/m give $u \approx 2.998\times10^8$ m/s
The historical moment

That number was already known — it was the measured speed of light. Two constants from benchtop electrostatics and magnetostatics experiments, combined, predicted it. That is what identified light as an electromagnetic wave.

Dropping to the phasor domain

The wave equation still carries two derivatives in time. Almost everything in RF is time-harmonic — driven at a single frequency $\omega$ — and for that case the time dependence can be removed entirely.

Write each field as a complex amplitude carrying an $e^{j\omega t}$ factor that we agree to keep implicit:

$$\vE(x,y,z,t) = \operatorname{Re}\left\{ \tilde{\vE}(x,y,z)\,e^{j\omega t} \right\}$$ the tilde marks a phasor: a complex amplitude with the time dependence factored out

Every time derivative now becomes a multiplication, because $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}e^{j\omega t} = j\omega e^{j\omega t}$:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial t} \;\longrightarrow\; j\omega \qquad\qquad \frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2} \;\longrightarrow\; -\omega^2$$

Substituting into the wave equation turns a partial differential equation in four variables into one in three:

$$\nabla^2 \tilde{\vE} + \omega^2\mu\varepsilon\,\tilde{\vE} = 0 \qquad\text{or}\qquad \nabla^2 \tilde{\vE} - \gamma^2 \tilde{\vE} = 0$$ the Helmholtz equation, with $\gamma^2 = -\omega^2\mu\varepsilon$ in a lossless medium

This is the single most useful move in the subject, and it is worth being clear about what it costs. You give up the ability to describe transients — switching a source on, a pulse, a step. What you gain is that every equation becomes algebraic in $j\omega$, and impedance becomes a meaningful concept. The whole of transmission-line theory lives in this domain.

Full derivation — including a lossy medium

If the medium has conductivity $\sigma$, a conduction current $\vJ = \sigma\vE$ flows wherever there is a field, and Ampère's law keeps that term:

$$\curl \tilde{\vH} = \sigma\tilde{\vE} + j\omega\varepsilon\tilde{\vE} = (\sigma + j\omega\varepsilon)\,\tilde{\vE}$$

Repeating the double-curl derivation with this right-hand side gives

$$\nabla^2\tilde{\vE} = j\omega\mu(\sigma + j\omega\varepsilon)\,\tilde{\vE}$$

so the propagation constant in general is

$$\gamma = \sqrt{j\omega\mu(\sigma + j\omega\varepsilon)} = \alpha + j\beta$$

It is complex, and both parts have a job. The real part $\alpha$ is the attenuation constant in nepers per metre — how fast the amplitude decays. The imaginary part $\beta$ is the phase constant in radians per metre — how fast the phase advances. Setting $\sigma = 0$ recovers the lossless case, where $\alpha = 0$ and $\beta = \omega\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon}$.

Keep the shape of this result in view. When the telegrapher's equations arrive in chapter 04 they produce $\gamma = \sqrt{(R + j\omega L)(G + j\omega C)}$ — the same structure, with circuit quantities in place of material ones. That is not a coincidence, and the parallel is the point of the next few chapters.

The simplest solution

Take a wave travelling in $+z$ with its electric field along $x$. Helmholtz reduces to an ordinary differential equation in one variable:

$$\frac{d^2 \tilde{E}_x}{dz^2} - \gamma^2 \tilde{E}_x = 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \tilde{E}_x(z) = E^+ e^{-\gamma z} + E^- e^{+\gamma z}$$

Two exponentials: one decaying in $+z$, one in $-z$. A forward wave and a backward wave. Every result in the rest of this site is a consequence of those two terms and what happens where they meet a boundary.

In the lossless case $\gamma = j\beta$, and restoring the time factor turns the forward term into

$$E_x(z,t) = \operatorname{Re}\left\{E^+ e^{-j\beta z}e^{j\omega t}\right\} = |E^+|\cos(\omega t - \beta z + \phi)$$

A point of constant phase satisfies $\omega t - \beta z = \text{const}$, so it moves at $z = \omega t/\beta$ — giving the phase velocity $u_p = \omega/\beta$ and the wavelength $\lambda = 2\pi/\beta$.

Two features of that picture are worth naming, because both carry into transmission lines unchanged. The fields are mutually perpendicular and both transverse to the direction of travel — a TEM wave. And in a lossless medium they are exactly in phase, their ratio being a real constant:

$$\eta = \frac{\tilde{E}_x}{\tilde{H}_y} = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{\varepsilon}} \qquad\qquad \eta_0 = \sqrt{\frac{\mu_0}{\varepsilon_0}} \approx 377\ \Omega$$ the intrinsic impedance of the medium — a property of the material, not of the wave
Remember this shape

A ratio of field quantities, fixed by the medium, with units of ohms, and the same for every wave travelling through it. When $Z_0 = \sqrt{L/C}$ appears on a transmission line in chapter 05, it is this same idea wearing circuit clothing — and it is why a line has a characteristic impedance at all.

Full derivation — where η comes from

Take the forward wave $\tilde{\vE} = \hat{x}\,E^+e^{-j\beta z}$ and feed it through Faraday's law in phasor form, $\curl\tilde{\vE} = -j\omega\mu\tilde{\vH}$. For a field with only an $x$ component varying only in $z$, the curl has a single surviving term:

$$\curl \tilde{\vE} = \hat{y}\,\frac{\partial \tilde{E}_x}{\partial z} = -\hat{y}\,j\beta E^+ e^{-j\beta z}$$

Setting that equal to $-j\omega\mu\tilde{\vH}$ gives

$$\tilde{H}_y = \frac{\beta}{\omega\mu}E^+e^{-j\beta z}$$

The magnetic field points along $\hat{y}$ — perpendicular to $\vE$ and to the direction of travel, exactly as claimed. Now substitute $\beta = \omega\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon}$:

$$\frac{\tilde{E}_x}{\tilde{H}_y} = \frac{\omega\mu}{\beta} = \frac{\omega\mu}{\omega\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon}} = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{\varepsilon}} = \eta$$

Because $\eta$ came out real, the two fields peak at the same instant. In a lossy medium $\gamma = \alpha + j\beta$ makes $\eta$ complex, the fields fall out of phase, and the wave carries less real power than their product would suggest — the same bookkeeping as a reactive load in a circuit.

How far out of phase takes no extra information. Repeating the step above without assuming $\gamma$ imaginary gives $\tilde{H}_y = (\gamma/j\omega\mu)\tilde{E}_x$, so $\eta\gamma = j\omega\mu$ exactly — and since $j\omega\mu$ has an angle of $90^\circ$,

$$\angle\eta = 90^\circ - \angle\gamma = 90^\circ - \arctan\frac{\beta}{\alpha}$$

which is what the figure's $\angle\eta$ readout reports as you move the attenuation slider. At $\alpha = 0$ it is zero, recovering the in-phase case. At the other extreme, a good conductor has $\alpha \to \beta$, giving $\angle\eta \to 45^\circ$ — the largest lag any passive medium produces, and the reason a wave entering metal is so quickly spent.

Self-check

Three questions on what the derivation actually assumed.

1. In the double-curl derivation, the term $\nabla(\dive \vE)$ was dropped. What justified that?

It was an assumption, not an identity. Gauss's law gives $\dive\vD = \rho_v$, so only with no free charge and uniform $\varepsilon$ does $\dive\vE$ vanish. Inside a medium whose permittivity varies with position that term survives and the field components remain coupled — which is why graded-index and inhomogeneous problems are much harder.

2. What is genuinely given up by moving to the phasor domain?

For a steady sinusoid the phasor result is exact, not approximate, and loss is handled fine by letting $\gamma$ be complex. What is lost is everything before steady state: switch-on, pulses, steps. Those need either the time-domain equations or a superposition of many frequencies.

3. The intrinsic impedance of free space is about 377 Ω. What does that number describe?

It is a ratio of field quantities, $E/H$, which happens to carry units of ohms. It dissipates nothing — free space is lossless, and $\eta$ being real is precisely the statement that E and H stay in phase. Antenna impedance is a related but distinct idea, and the 377 Ω figure is a property of the medium alone.
