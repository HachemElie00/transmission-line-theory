Chapter 10

The Smith chart

Not a nomogram, not a lookup table, and not a relic of the era before calculators. It is the impedance plane redrawn on the $\Gamma$ disc — and once you see which curves are circles and why, the whole thing becomes obvious rather than intimidating.

Assumes: chapters 06 and 08 — $\Gamma$, its rotation, and input impedance.

The problem it solves

Everything so far has moved between two descriptions of the same thing. Impedance is what components have; $\Gamma$ is what waves do. The map between them is the Möbius pair from chapter 06, in normalised form $z = Z/Z_0$:

$$\Gamma = \frac{z-1}{z+1} \qquad\qquad z = \frac{1+\Gamma}{1-\Gamma}$$

Each description makes different things easy, and that is the whole difficulty.

EasyAwkward
Impedanceadding components in series, reading off $R$ and $X$moving along a line — that tangent formula
$\Gamma$moving along a line — just a rotationseeing what impedance you are actually at

Smith's idea was to stop choosing. Draw the impedance grid onto the $\Gamma$ plane, and both become easy at once: travel along the line is still a plain rotation, and you can read the impedance directly off the grid you are standing on.

Why the grid is made of circles

Write $\Gamma = u + jv$ and $z = r + jx$, and push the second formula through. Separating real and imaginary parts gives

$$r = \frac{1-u^2-v^2}{(1-u)^2+v^2} \qquad\qquad x = \frac{2v}{(1-u)^2+v^2}$$

Now ask: for a fixed $r$, what curve do $u$ and $v$ trace? Clear the denominator, collect terms and complete the square, and a circle appears — exactly, not approximately.

$$\textbf{constant } r: \quad \left(u - \frac{r}{1+r}\right)^2 + v^2 = \left(\frac{1}{1+r}\right)^2$$ $$\textbf{constant } x: \quad \left(u - 1\right)^2 + \left(v - \frac{1}{x}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{1}{x}\right)^2$$
Full derivation — completing the square

Take the expression for $r$ and multiply out:

$$r\left[(1-u)^2 + v^2\right] = 1 - u^2 - v^2$$
$$r - 2ru + ru^2 + rv^2 = 1 - u^2 - v^2$$

Gather every term in $u$ and $v$ on the left:

$$u^2(1+r) - 2ru + v^2(1+r) = 1 - r$$

Divide by $(1+r)$ and complete the square in $u$:

$$u^2 - \frac{2r}{1+r}u + \left(\frac{r}{1+r}\right)^2 + v^2 = \frac{1-r}{1+r} + \left(\frac{r}{1+r}\right)^2$$

The right-hand side collapses. Putting it over $(1+r)^2$ gives $\left[(1-r)(1+r) + r^2\right]/(1+r)^2 = 1/(1+r)^2$, so

$$\left(u - \frac{r}{1+r}\right)^2 + v^2 = \frac{1}{(1+r)^2}$$

which is a circle centred on the real axis at $r/(1+r)$ with radius $1/(1+r)$. The same treatment of the $x$ expression gives the second family. Note that every constant-$r$ circle passes through $\Gamma = +1$, since $u=1,\,v=0$ satisfies the equation for any $r$ — that point is $z = \infty$, where all the resistance circles must meet.

There is a deeper reason none of this required luck. The map $\Gamma = (z-1)/(z+1)$ is a Möbius transformation, and such maps always send circles and straight lines to circles and straight lines. The impedance grid is a set of straight lines; its image had to be circles.

The chart itself

Drag anywhere inside the circle to set a load. The dashed circle through your point is the constant-SWR circle: the path the impedance follows as you move along the line, because $|\Gamma|$ never changes on a lossless line.

Sweep the slider and watch three facts from earlier chapters become one motion. The point travels clockwise; it returns to the start after half a wavelength; and it stays on a circle of constant radius, because a lossless line cannot change $|\Gamma|$. Where that circle crosses the real axis on the left you are at a voltage minimum and $z = 1/S$; where it crosses on the right you are at a maximum and $z = S$.

Reading SWR straight off the chart

The constant-SWR circle crosses the positive real axis at exactly $r = S$. So you never compute the standing wave ratio here — you look at where the circle cuts the right-hand half of the horizontal line and read it.

The admittance overlay

Matching networks add components in parallel as often as in series, and parallel elements add admittances, not impedances. Working in $y = 1/z$ ought to need a second chart. It does not.

Chapter 09 showed that a quarter-wave line inverts a normalised impedance. In $\Gamma$ terms a quarter wave is a half rotation, so:

$$y = \frac{1}{z} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \Gamma_y = -\Gamma_z$$ taking the reciprocal of an impedance is a 180° rotation on the chart — nothing more

That minus sign has to be applied somewhere, and there are two places to put it. Both are in common use, and they are the same statement written two ways.

Turn the point, or turn the grid

Turn the point. Move your point half a turn about the centre and go on reading the ordinary impedance grid. What you read off it is now $g$ and $b$, and the circle marked $r = 1$ is the one to aim at when you want $g = 1$. This is what you do on a plain printed chart, and it is the version most people are taught.

Turn the grid. Leave the point alone and lay a second grid, rotated half a turn, over the first; read the same point against whichever grid you need. This is what a commercial immittance chart prints, and what the overlay button below does.

Either is correct. Doing both is not — turn the point and read it against a turned grid and the minus sign has been applied twice, which puts you back at $z$.

Nothing physical moves in either case. $\Gamma$ is one number, fixed by the load and by where you are standing on the line. What changes is the bookkeeping: in the first version the mark on your paper stops being $\Gamma$ and becomes $-\Gamma$, a device for reusing the impedance circles to read admittance; in the second it stays $\Gamma$ and you change rulers. The workbench can do it either way, and says which it is doing.

Full derivation — one line

Substitute $z = 1/y$ into the definition and multiply through by $y$:

$$\Gamma_z = \frac{1/y - 1}{1/y + 1} = \frac{1 - y}{1 + y} = -\frac{y-1}{y+1} = -\Gamma_y$$

So the admittance coordinate of a point is the negation of its impedance coordinate — a rotation by $\pi$ about the origin. In practice this means one of two equivalent things, and it is worth being clear which you are doing:

Rotate the point, keep the grid. Move your point half a turn and read the ordinary impedance grid; the numbers you read are now $g$ and $b$.

Rotate the grid, keep the point. Overlay a second grid rotated 180° and read your point against it. This is what the button below does, and what a printed immittance chart shows: both grids at once, so a matching problem needs no rotation at all.

Turn on the admittance overlay above. The added grid is the same set of circles rotated half a turn, and the two together are what a commercial immittance chart prints. Series elements move you along the impedance circles; shunt elements move you along the admittance ones.

A worked case, all of it read rather than computed. Take $Z_L = 100 - j70\ \Omega$ on a 50 Ω line, so $z_L = 2 - j1.4$ — the fourth preset above.

  1. Plot it. Find the $r = 2$ circle, follow it to where it meets $x = -1.4$, in the lower half because the load is capacitive.
  2. Read $\Gamma$. The vector from the centre gives $|\Gamma| \approx 0.52$ at about $-29°$, so roughly 27 % of the power is reflected.
  3. Read SWR. Follow the constant-radius circle round to the positive real axis: it cuts at about $r = 3.2$, so $S \approx 3.2$.
  4. Find the first voltage minimum. Sweep clockwise to the negative real axis — about $0.21\lambda$ from the load. There $z = 1/S \approx 0.32$, a pure resistance of about 16 Ω.

Every one of those is checkable against the figure above: load the fourth preset, and the readouts show $\Gamma = 0.520\angle{-29°}$ and $S = 3.16$ at the load. Sweep to $0.209\lambda$ and the point lands on the negative real axis with $z = 0.32$.

Four numbers, no algebra. That is the argument for the chart, and it is why it survived the calculator: it is not faster arithmetic, it is a picture of the whole solution space at once, in which you can see where a match is and how to get there.

The chart above is deliberately stripped down, so that the geometry stays visible. For a full one — every grid line, the wavelength and angle scales round the rim, and the radially scaled parameters underneath — there is a workbench at the end of the site.

The one thing to hold onto

A point inside a disc, turning clockwise on a fixed circle as you walk back along the line, one lap per half wavelength. Everything else — the grid, the overlay, the scales round the rim — is bookkeeping laid on top of that single motion.

Self-check

Three questions on what the chart is.

1. Why is the Smith chart bounded by a circle rather than extending forever?

The chart's coordinates are $\Gamma$, not impedance. A passive load cannot reflect more than it receives, so $|\Gamma| \le 1$ and every possible load falls inside the unit disc. That is the chart's real trick: it maps the infinite impedance half-plane into a finite disc, with $z = \infty$ landing on a single point at the right-hand edge. Active devices can have $|\Gamma| > 1$, and their points fall outside — which is why oscillator design uses a compressed chart.

2. What does rotating a point 180° about the centre do?

Substituting $z = 1/y$ gives $\Gamma_z = -\Gamma_y$ exactly, so a half turn is the reciprocal. The first option is not wrong so much as incomplete — a quarter-wave line is a half rotation, and chapter 09 showed a quarter-wave line inverts the impedance. They are the same fact. Simply negating the reactance would be a reflection in the real axis, not a rotation.

3. Moving along a lossless line traces what path on the chart?

$\Gamma(d) = \Gamma_L e^{-j2\beta d}$ has constant magnitude, so the radius is fixed and the motion is a circle. Clockwise toward the generator, completing a lap when $2\beta d = 2\pi$, that is $d = \lambda/2$. The inward spiral is what a lossy line gives, since then $|\Gamma|$ really does shrink — and a long enough lossy line looks matched from the input no matter what is on the end.
