Chapter 06

Reflection at a load

A line supports one wave in each direction. Until now the backward one has had no reason to exist. Put something on the end that the forward wave alone cannot satisfy, and it appears — not as a defect, but because the algebra has no other solution.

Assumes: chapter 04 — the two-wave solution, and $\tilde V^\pm/\tilde I^\pm = \pm Z_0$.

Why a wave comes back

Chapter 04 gave the general solution on a lossless line. Put the load at $z = 0$, with the line running off in the $-z$ direction toward the generator:

$$\tilde{V}(z) = V_0^+e^{-j\beta z} + V_0^-e^{+j\beta z} \qquad \tilde{I}(z) = \frac{V_0^+}{Z_0}e^{-j\beta z} - \frac{V_0^-}{Z_0}e^{+j\beta z}$$

At the load, $z = 0$, both exponentials become 1:

$$\tilde{V}(0) = V_0^+ + V_0^- \qquad\qquad \tilde{I}(0) = \frac{V_0^+ - V_0^-}{Z_0}$$

The load imposes one condition and one only: the ratio of voltage to current at that point must equal $Z_L$. Nothing else about the load matters.

$$Z_L = \frac{\tilde{V}(0)}{\tilde{I}(0)} = Z_0\,\frac{V_0^+ + V_0^-}{V_0^+ - V_0^-}$$

Now look at what that demands. Suppose $V_0^- = 0$ — only a forward wave. Then the ratio is forced to be $Z_0$ exactly, whatever the load happens to be. So unless $Z_L = Z_0$, a single wave cannot satisfy the boundary condition, and the only remaining freedom is $V_0^-$. It takes whatever value makes the equation true.

The same argument as the prologue

In part two of the prologue, a single plane wave forced $E/H = \eta_1$ while the medium beyond the boundary demanded $\eta_2$, so a reflected wave had to exist. This is that argument again with the field quantities replaced by circuit ones — and the minus sign in the current is once more what makes the escape possible.

The reflection coefficient

Solve that equation for the ratio of the two waves and the result is compact:

$$\Gamma = \frac{V_0^-}{V_0^+} = \frac{Z_L - Z_0}{Z_L + Z_0}$$ complex in general — a load can shift phase as well as shrink amplitude
Full derivation — three lines of algebra

Start from the boundary condition and cross-multiply:

$$Z_L(V_0^+ - V_0^-) = Z_0(V_0^+ + V_0^-)$$

Collect the forward terms on one side and the backward terms on the other:

$$V_0^+(Z_L - Z_0) = V_0^-(Z_L + Z_0)$$

Divide:

$$\frac{V_0^-}{V_0^+} = \frac{Z_L - Z_0}{Z_L + Z_0} = \Gamma$$

Two remarks. First, dividing by $Z_L + Z_0$ is safe for any passive load, since both have non-negative real parts and cannot sum to zero. Second, the expression is often cleaner in normalised form. Writing $z_L = Z_L/Z_0$ and dividing top and bottom by $Z_0$:

$$\Gamma = \frac{z_L - 1}{z_L + 1} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad z_L = \frac{1+\Gamma}{1-\Gamma}$$

That pair is a Möbius transformation between the impedance plane and the $\Gamma$ plane, and it is invertible: every impedance has exactly one $\Gamma$ and vice versa. The Smith chart in chapter 10 is nothing more than this map drawn out.

Read the formula as a comparison. The numerator is how far the load is from the line; the denominator normalises it. A load equal to $Z_0$ gives zero. A load very different from $Z_0$ gives something approaching unit magnitude.

The last row is worth dwelling on. A capacitor or inductor cannot absorb average power, so it must send every watt back — hence $|\Gamma| = 1$, exactly as for a short or an open. What distinguishes them is only the phase, and that phase is what shifts the pattern along the line.

Γ on the complex plane

Because $|\Gamma| \le 1$ for any passive load, every possible $\Gamma$ lives inside the unit circle. That disc is the natural home of this subject, and it is where the Smith chart will be built.

Move the load around and note which regions of the disc it reaches. Purely resistive loads land on the real axis: those greater than $Z_0$ to the right of the origin, those smaller to the left. Purely reactive loads land on the unit circle itself — full reflection, phase set by how reactive. Everything else fills the interior.

Power, and what reflection costs

Power in a travelling wave goes as amplitude squared, so the fraction reflected is $|\Gamma|^2$ and the fraction delivered is whatever is left:

$$\frac{P_{\text{refl}}}{P_{\text{inc}}} = |\Gamma|^2 \qquad\qquad \frac{P_{\text{deliv}}}{P_{\text{inc}}} = 1 - |\Gamma|^2$$ on a lossless line, with no loss the two must account for everything

Engineers usually quote this logarithmically, as return loss: how far below the incident wave the reflected one sits.

$$\text{RL} = -20\log_{10}|\Gamma| \quad\text{dB}$$

The current wave, and a transmission coefficient above one

$\Gamma$ was defined on voltage. The current waves carry the opposite sign, $\tilde{I}^- = -V_0^-/Z_0$, so the reflection coefficient for current is $-\Gamma$. At an open circuit the voltage reflects with $+1$ and doubles; the current reflects with $-1$ and cancels to zero, as an open demands.

The total voltage at the load, over the incident voltage, is the transmission coefficient, and it can exceed one:

$$\tau = \frac{\tilde{V}(0)}{V_0^+} = 1 + \Gamma \qquad\qquad \frac{\tilde{I}(0)}{V_0^+/Z_0} = 1 - \Gamma$$ an open load has $\tau = 2$: twice the incident voltage, and no energy is created

The resolution is that power needs current as well as voltage. The power delivered to the load is the product of the two factors above, and the cross terms are purely imaginary:

$$P_L = \tfrac12\operatorname{Re}\{\tilde{V}(0)\tilde{I}(0)^*\} = \frac{|V_0^+|^2}{2Z_0}\operatorname{Re}\{(1+\Gamma)(1-\Gamma^*)\} = \frac{|V_0^+|^2}{2Z_0}\left(1 - |\Gamma|^2\right)$$

since $(1+\Gamma)(1-\Gamma^*) = 1 - |\Gamma|^2 + (\Gamma - \Gamma^*)$ and $\Gamma - \Gamma^*$ is imaginary. Where the voltage rises the current falls, and the product comes out exactly as the power balance requires. Part two met the same thing at a boundary between two media: there $\tau$ exceeds one whenever the second medium has the higher impedance.

Looking from further up the line

$\Gamma$ was defined at the load. Stand a distance $l$ back toward the generator and the two waves have travelled different distances to reach you — the reflected one has gone out and come back, an extra $2l$. $\Gamma$ is a ratio of the two waves, so only that difference matters, and it gives:

$$\Gamma(l) = \Gamma_L\,e^{-j2\beta l}$$ the magnitude never changes on a lossless line; only the angle turns

Three consequences follow immediately, and all three shape everything after this chapter.

$|e^{-j2\beta l}| = 1$$|\Gamma|$ is the same everywhere on a lossless line, so SWR is a property of the whole line

The locus spirals inward instead of circling. A long enough lossy line looks matched from the input regardless of what is on the end — which is true, and is why a badly terminated long cable can still measure well.

This is the Smith chart

A point inside the unit disc, moving clockwise round a circle of fixed radius as you walk back along the line, one lap per half wavelength. Chapter 10 adds the impedance grid on top; the motion is already fully determined here.

When the source is mismatched too

So far the generator has been taken for granted, as though it launched a fixed $V_0^+$ and absorbed whatever came back. A real one has an internal impedance $Z_g$, and if $Z_g \ne Z_0$ the reflected wave reflects again at the source, with

$$\Gamma_g = \frac{Z_g - Z_0}{Z_g + Z_0}$$

and the forward wave on the line is the sum of the first launch and every echo of it. The echoes form a geometric series, and it sums in closed form:

$$V_0^+ = \frac{V_g Z_0}{Z_g + Z_0}\cdot\frac{1}{1 - \Gamma_g\Gamma_L e^{-j2\beta l}}$$ the first factor is the launched wave; the second sums the round trips, each multiplying by $\Gamma_g\Gamma_L e^{-j2\beta l}$
Full derivation — the series, and the power that reaches the load

Put the load at $z = 0$ and the source at $z = -l$. The first wave launched into the line, seen at the source end, is $V_gZ_0/(Z_g + Z_0)$, since the line presents $Z_0$ to the source before any reflection returns. One round trip later it comes back reduced by $\Gamma_L$, delayed by a phase $2\beta l$, and is reflected again by $\Gamma_g$, joining the forward wave. Summing all of them,

$$V^+_{\text{at source}} = \frac{V_gZ_0}{Z_g + Z_0}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\Gamma_g\Gamma_Le^{-j2\beta l}\right)^n = \frac{V_gZ_0}{Z_g + Z_0}\cdot\frac{1}{1 - \Gamma_g\Gamma_Le^{-j2\beta l}}$$

which converges because $|\Gamma_g\Gamma_L| < 1$ unless both ends reflect totally. The same answer comes from solving the boundary conditions at both ends at once, without any series; the series is the time-domain picture of chapter 03's bounce diagram, summed in steady state.

The power reaching the load follows from the previous section, $P_L = |V_0^+|^2(1 - |\Gamma_L|^2)/2Z_0$. Divided by the most the source could ever deliver, its available power $|V_g|^2/8R_g$, it takes a symmetric form:

$$\frac{P_L}{P_{\text{av}}} = \frac{\left(1 - |\Gamma_g|^2\right)\left(1 - |\Gamma_L|^2\right)}{\left|1 - \Gamma_g\Gamma_Le^{-j2\beta l}\right|^2}$$

Three things show here. With either end matched the ripple vanishes and the length stops mattering. With both mismatched, the delivered power swings between two limits as the echoes add in and out of step. And the top of the swing reaches the full available power only when $|\Gamma_g| = |\Gamma_L|$: a length of line can then turn the load into the conjugate of the source, which is chapter 08's conjugate match reached by choosing $l$ alone.

When Z0 is complex, |Γ| can exceed one

The statement that $|\Gamma| \le 1$ for every passive load rests on $Z_0$ being real. On a lossy line at low frequency it is not (chapter 04), and then the bound fails. Take $Z_0 = 40 - j30\ \Omega$ and a pure reactance $Z_L = j30\ \Omega$:

$$\Gamma = \frac{j30 - (40 - j30)}{j30 + (40 - j30)} = \frac{-40 + j60}{40} = -1 + j1.5, \qquad |\Gamma| = 1.80$$

The load is passive, yet the reflected wave is larger than the incident one. Nothing is wrong: with a complex $Z_0$ the power in the two waves is no longer $|V^+|^2$ and $|V^-|^2$ separately, because the incident and reflected waves exchange power through cross terms. $\Gamma$ still correctly gives the ratio of the voltage waves; it just no longer measures power.

The cure is to define waves that do measure power, Kurokawa's power waves, referred to an impedance $Z_r$ with positive real part:

$$a = \frac{V + Z_rI}{2\sqrt{\operatorname{Re}Z_r}}, \qquad b = \frac{V - Z_r^*I}{2\sqrt{\operatorname{Re}Z_r}}, \qquad \Gamma_p = \frac{b}{a} = \frac{Z_L - Z_r^*}{Z_L + Z_r}$$ the power delivered is exactly $|a|^2 - |b|^2$, so $|\Gamma_p| \le 1$ for every passive load, whatever $Z_r$

For the example above, $\Gamma_p = (j30 - 40 - j30)/(j30 + 40 - j30) = -1$: magnitude exactly one, as a lossless load requires. When $Z_r$ is real, $Z_r^* = Z_r$ and $\Gamma_p$ reduces to the familiar $\Gamma$, which is why the distinction never arises on the lossless lines of the rest of this site. It matters for lossy lines, and it is one of the two definitions on which the scattering parameters of microwave networks are built.

Reflections in time: what a TDR sees

A time-domain reflectometer sends a fast step down a line from a matched source and records the voltage at its own end. Until the reflection returns it sees only the launched step; after a round trip of $2t_d$ it sees the step plus whatever the load sent back. A resistor sends back a step scaled by $\Gamma$. A reactive load sends back something that changes with time, and its shape names the load:

An inductor carries no current at first, so it looks like an open: the reflection starts at $+1$ and relaxes to $\Gamma_R$ with time constant $L/(R + Z_0)$. A capacitor holds its voltage at first, so it looks like a short: the reflection starts at $-1$ and relaxes with time constant $C\,RZ_0/(R + Z_0)$. The size and direction of each bump and dip along a real cable read off, in this way, the inductive connector, the capacitive pad, the resistive end, and from the time at which each appears, where it is.

Self-check

Three questions on what Γ encodes.

1. Why does a purely reactive load give $|\Gamma| = 1$?

An ideal inductor or capacitor stores energy and gives it all back over a cycle. With no dissipation the reflected power must equal the incident power, so $|\Gamma|^2 = 1$. What distinguishes a capacitor from an inductor from a short is purely the phase of $\Gamma$ — and that phase determines where the standing-wave pattern sits.

2. A load gives $|\Gamma| = 0.316$. How much power reaches it?

$|\Gamma|$ is an amplitude ratio; power goes as its square. $0.316^2 = 0.1$, so 10 % reflects and 90 % is delivered — a 10 dB return loss. The phase does not enter: it controls where the standing-wave maxima sit, not how much power gets through.

3. Moving back along a lossless line, what changes about $\Gamma$?

$\Gamma(l) = \Gamma_L e^{-j2\beta l}$, and the exponential has unit magnitude, so $|\Gamma|$ is constant everywhere on a lossless line. The angle decreases at rate $2\beta$, giving a full rotation when $2\beta l = 2\pi$, that is $l = \lambda/2$. On a lossy line the magnitude does shrink and the locus spirals inward — but that is loss, not distance as such.
