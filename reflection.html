Chapter 06

Reflection at a load

A line supports one wave in each direction. Until now the backward one has had no reason to exist. Put something on the end that the forward wave alone cannot satisfy, and it appears — not as a defect, but because the algebra has no other solution.

Assumes: chapter 04 — the two-wave solution, and $\tilde V^\pm/\tilde I^\pm = \pm Z_0$.

Why a wave comes back

Chapter 04 gave the general solution on a lossless line. Put the load at $z = 0$, with the line running off in the $-z$ direction toward the generator:

$$\tilde{V}(z) = V_0^+e^{-j\beta z} + V_0^-e^{+j\beta z} \qquad \tilde{I}(z) = \frac{V_0^+}{Z_0}e^{-j\beta z} - \frac{V_0^-}{Z_0}e^{+j\beta z}$$

At the load, $z = 0$, both exponentials become 1:

$$\tilde{V}(0) = V_0^+ + V_0^- \qquad\qquad \tilde{I}(0) = \frac{V_0^+ - V_0^-}{Z_0}$$

The load imposes one condition and one only: the ratio of voltage to current at that point must equal $Z_L$. Nothing else about the load matters.

$$Z_L = \frac{\tilde{V}(0)}{\tilde{I}(0)} = Z_0\,\frac{V_0^+ + V_0^-}{V_0^+ - V_0^-}$$

Now look at what that demands. Suppose $V_0^- = 0$ — only a forward wave. Then the ratio is forced to be $Z_0$ exactly, whatever the load happens to be. So unless $Z_L = Z_0$, a single wave cannot satisfy the boundary condition, and the only remaining freedom is $V_0^-$. It takes whatever value makes the equation true.

The same argument as the prologue

In chapter 01 of the prologue, a single travelling wave forced $V/I = Z_0$ and the load demanded something else, so a second wave had to exist. This is that argument again with the field quantities replaced by circuit ones — and the minus sign in the current is once more what makes the escape possible.

The reflection coefficient

Solve that equation for the ratio of the two waves and the result is compact:

$$\Gamma = \frac{V_0^-}{V_0^+} = \frac{Z_L - Z_0}{Z_L + Z_0}$$ complex in general — a load can shift phase as well as shrink amplitude
Full derivation — three lines of algebra

Start from the boundary condition and cross-multiply:

$$Z_L(V_0^+ - V_0^-) = Z_0(V_0^+ + V_0^-)$$

Collect the forward terms on one side and the backward terms on the other:

$$V_0^+(Z_L - Z_0) = V_0^-(Z_L + Z_0)$$

Divide:

$$\frac{V_0^-}{V_0^+} = \frac{Z_L - Z_0}{Z_L + Z_0} = \Gamma$$

Two remarks. First, dividing by $Z_L + Z_0$ is safe for any passive load, since both have non-negative real parts and cannot sum to zero. Second, the expression is often cleaner in normalised form. Writing $z_L = Z_L/Z_0$ and dividing top and bottom by $Z_0$:

$$\Gamma = \frac{z_L - 1}{z_L + 1} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad z_L = \frac{1+\Gamma}{1-\Gamma}$$

That pair is a Möbius transformation between the impedance plane and the $\Gamma$ plane, and it is invertible: every impedance has exactly one $\Gamma$ and vice versa. The Smith chart in chapter 10 is nothing more than this map drawn out.

Read the formula as a comparison. The numerator is how far the load is from the line; the denominator normalises it. A load equal to $Z_0$ gives zero. A load very different from $Z_0$ gives something approaching unit magnitude.

The last row is worth dwelling on. A capacitor or inductor cannot absorb average power, so it must send every watt back — hence $|\Gamma| = 1$, exactly as for a short or an open. What distinguishes them is only the phase, and that phase is what shifts the pattern along the line.

Γ on the complex plane

Because $|\Gamma| \le 1$ for any passive load, every possible $\Gamma$ lives inside the unit circle. That disc is the natural home of this subject, and it is where the Smith chart will be built.

Move the load around and note which regions of the disc it reaches. Purely resistive loads land on the real axis: those greater than $Z_0$ to the right of the origin, those smaller to the left. Purely reactive loads land on the unit circle itself — full reflection, phase set by how reactive. Everything else fills the interior.

Power, and what reflection costs

Power in a travelling wave goes as amplitude squared, so the fraction reflected is $|\Gamma|^2$ and the fraction delivered is whatever is left:

$$\frac{P_{\text{refl}}}{P_{\text{inc}}} = |\Gamma|^2 \qquad\qquad \frac{P_{\text{deliv}}}{P_{\text{inc}}} = 1 - |\Gamma|^2$$ on a lossless line, with no loss the two must account for everything

Engineers usually quote this logarithmically, as return loss: how far below the incident wave the reflected one sits.

$$\text{RL} = -20\log_{10}|\Gamma| \quad\text{dB}$$

Looking from further up the line

$\Gamma$ was defined at the load. Stand a distance $l$ back toward the generator and the two waves have travelled different distances to reach you — the reflected one has gone out and come back, an extra $2l$. Chapter 02 showed why only that difference matters, and it gives:

$$\Gamma(l) = \Gamma_L\,e^{-j2\beta l}$$ the magnitude never changes on a lossless line; only the angle turns

Three consequences follow immediately, and all three shape everything after this chapter.

BecauseIt follows that
$|e^{-j2\beta l}| = 1$$|\Gamma|$ is the same everywhere on a lossless line, so SWR is a property of the whole line

On a lossy line the picture changes in one way only: $\Gamma(l) = \Gamma_L e^{-2\gamma l}$ carries a real part too, so the magnitude shrinks as you move back. The locus spirals inward instead of circling. A long enough lossy line looks matched from the input regardless of what is on the end — which is true, and is why a badly terminated long cable can still measure well.

This is the Smith chart

A point inside the unit disc, moving clockwise round a circle of fixed radius as you walk back along the line, one lap per half wavelength. Chapter 10 adds the impedance grid on top; the motion is already fully determined here.

Self-check

Three questions on what Γ encodes.

1. Why does a purely reactive load give $|\Gamma| = 1$?

An ideal inductor or capacitor stores energy and gives it all back over a cycle. With no dissipation the reflected power must equal the incident power, so $|\Gamma|^2 = 1$. What distinguishes a capacitor from an inductor from a short is purely the phase of $\Gamma$ — and that phase determines where the standing-wave pattern sits.

2. A load gives $|\Gamma| = 0.316$. How much power reaches it?

$|\Gamma|$ is an amplitude ratio; power goes as its square. $0.316^2 = 0.1$, so 10 % reflects and 90 % is delivered — a 10 dB return loss. The phase does not enter: it controls where the standing-wave maxima sit, not how much power gets through.

3. Moving back along a lossless line, what changes about $\Gamma$?

$\Gamma(l) = \Gamma_L e^{-j2\beta l}$, and the exponential has unit magnitude, so $|\Gamma|$ is constant everywhere on a lossless line. The angle decreases at rate $2\beta$, giving a full rotation when $2\beta l = 2\pi$, that is $l = \lambda/2$. On a lossy line the magnitude does shrink and the locus spirals inward — but that is loss, not distance as such.
